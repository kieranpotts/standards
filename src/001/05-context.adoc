= Context

The *context* part of a specification is descriptive. It establishes the problem space the system operates in and the domain the system is built around. None of it is a requirement in its own right; it is the shared frame of reference from which the requirements are derived.

== Overview

A high-level description of the system: its *mission* (the value it delivers and to whom), the *problem* it solves, and its *scope* (what is in, and — just as importantly — what is explicitly out). The scope statement is what stops the specification quietly expanding to describe systems and responsibilities that belong elsewhere.

== Constraints

The conditions imposed on the system from outside — the givens it must work within, rather than behaviors it chooses to exhibit. These include:

* *Regulatory and legal* constraints — applicable laws and standards (eg. data-protection law).
* *Business* constraints — contractual terms, commercial boundaries.
* *Assumptions* — standing assumptions the specification depends on. If an assumption proves false, the affected requirements must be revisited.
* *Dependencies* — external systems and services the system relies on.

Constraints are descriptive. Where a constraint implies a testable obligation on the system, state that obligation as a <<_behaviors, rule>> or a <<_qualities, quality>> and cross-reference it from the constraint, keeping the constraint itself purely a statement of the boundary.

== Glossary

The project's *ubiquitous language* — every domain term, each with a single agreed meaning, used consistently throughout the specification. Consistent vocabulary is what keeps the model, the actors, and the requirements coherent.

Keep each definition short. For terms that are also domain entities, define the term briefly in the glossary and link to the model for the full structure — the glossary points, the model defines. Using the same term to mean the same thing in the glossary, the model, and every scenario is one of the highest-leverage things a specification can get right.

== Model

The *domain model* documents the entities in the system's domain — the categories of information the system stores — their meaningful attributes, and how they relate to one another. An entity-relationship diagram may be embedded to summarize the model visually.

The model anchors the project's shared vocabulary and is the foundation from which the actors and features are derived. It is also, in practice, where the SRS comes closest to the software architecture: a sound domain model tends to become the backbone of the implementation. For modeling technique, see *link:../004/README.adoc[TS-4: Modeling]*.

The model declares the _states_ an entity can hold (eg. a `status` of `available`, `reserved`, or `sold`); the legal _transitions_ between those states are a prescriptive concern and belong in <<_behaviors, rules>>.

== Actors

The *actors* are the participants in the domain — the people, organizations, and external systems that have goals the system must serve. Actors describe _who_ participates, not _what_ each is permitted to do; permissions are specified separately, in <<_behaviors, access>>.

Actors are typically arranged as a *hierarchy*, ordered by privilege. Privileges are inherited down the hierarchy: an actor holds every capability of the actors below it, plus whatever is granted to it directly. Even small differences between actor types can have a significant impact on the system's design and the effort required to build it, so the hierarchy is worth stating explicitly.

The actors are derived from the domain model, and the complete map of which actor may exercise which capability is recorded in the <<_behaviors, access matrix>>.
