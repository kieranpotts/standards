= Ways of working with AI

There are three ways of working with AI tools on software. What separates them is
how correctness is established.

* In *vibe coding*, correctness is established by observation. The code runs, the
  output looks right, and that is the whole of the verification.

* In *AI-assisted development*, correctness is established by human inspection.
  The developer reads and reviews every change before accepting it.

* In *agentic development*, correctness is established by engineered
  verification. Checks are built into the workflow itself, so that agents have
  self-verifiable criteria for what "done" and "correct" mean.

This is the distinction that governs what can safely be built each way, and it is
the one to reason from. A second distinction cuts across it: the locus of
control, and whether the interaction loop is driven by the human or by the agent.
That one matters for tooling rather than for risk. AI-assisted work is best
served by harnesses embedded in traditional development tools, such as code
editors and IDEs, while agentic work is better served by an interface in which
the agent chat view takes precedence over the code view.

Of the three, only the latter two are treated at length in this standard. The
sections that follow — on choosing models and interfaces, engineering the
harness, tuning model behavior, and engineering context — supply the components
from which both are built, and each then gets a section of its own.

== Vibe coding

Vibe coding is improvisational and exploratory. The developer describes what they
want, accepts what comes back, and judges the result by whether it appears to
work. The code itself goes largely unread.

Because nothing verifies the output, nothing constrains it. That makes vibe
coding fast, and well suited to throwaway prototypes, internal scripts, and
research work with no clear end goal — and unsuitable for anything that has to be
production-grade. The section on human-in-the-loop sets out how to calibrate that
choice against a project's risk profile.

Vibe coding is not covered further here. Its defining characteristic is the
absence of engineering, so there is little to prescribe beyond knowing when it is
and is not appropriate.

== AI-assisted development

AI-assisted development uses agents for *augmentation*. The human developer
remains the primary driver, using AI as a tool for specific sub-tasks: writing a
function, explaining a code block, generating a test case. The developer manages
the loop manually — prompt, review, apply, iterate — and the interaction is
synchronous.

Correctness rests on the developer reading every change. This is a real
constraint, not an incidental one: the approach scales to the volume of code one
person can meaningfully review, and no further.

== Agentic development

Agentic workflows use agents for *delegation*. The human shifts from primary
implementer to supervisor. The agent is given a high-level goal and empowered to
manage its own internal loop. It plans its own steps, executes them with the
available tools, observes the results, and corrects course autonomously until the
goal is achieved. The human intervenes not to perform the work but to provide
steering, constraints, and final approval. The interaction is more asynchronous.

Because no human reads every line, agentic development can look like vibe coding
from the outside. The two could hardly be less alike. Vibe coding removes the
review step and puts nothing in its place. Agentic development replaces human
review with verification engineered into the workflow — guides, sensors,
deterministic checks, and explicit success criteria — and is, in consequence, the
most structured of the three ways of working, not the loosest. Autonomy is earned
against that machinery rather than assumed.

That machinery is also what lets agentic workflows scale past the volume of work
a human can personally inspect, which is the point of adopting them.
