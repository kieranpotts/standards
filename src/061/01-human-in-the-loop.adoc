= Human-in-the-loop

AI tools are most effective when directed by experienced software developers. They are much less effective — and, indeed, outright dangerous — when used as autonomous replacements for human decision-making.

Early academic research suggests that, the more skills and experience you have as a software developer, the better results you will get from working with AI coding tools. These tools amplify your existing knowledge rather than replace it. You need to remember fewer low-level details, such as the nuances of your programming language. This frees you up to focus on higher-level concerns like design, architecture, and problem-solving — the important stuff, and the stuff that you get better at with experience.

While modern AI models excel at generating code based on patterns in their training data, production-grade work still requires human skill, experience, and judgment. So too does managing complex projects, inventing novel solutions, and deciding on architectural tradeoffs.

Experienced software developers bring qualities that AI models lack, such as: judgment about long-term maintainability and technical debt tradeoffs; creativity and the ability to make unconventional semantic connections; understanding of how systems interact across multiple domains; the wisdom to push back on requirements that don't make sense; and context about why certain patterns were chosen in the past.

Like previous waves of programming automation — high-level languages, compilers, debuggers, and IDEs — AI tools automate many tasks, but managing a project's overall scope and implementation still falls to humans.

Coding agents are best used to enact human ideas, not as autonomous coding employees. They are at their best reducing clerical and repetitive work, freeing humans to concentrate on the judgment-heavy work that AI cannot do well.

The goal, then, is *AI-augmented software development* — humans directing AI tools, and firmly in charge of controlling the environments in which the AIs operate. What changes from one project to the next is not whether humans are in the loop, but the _degree_ of augmentation — how much of the work is handed off to AI agents, and how tightly that work is supervised.

There MUST always be a human-in-the-loop when using AI tools for software development. Only the _degree_ of human involvement may change with the domain and the risk profile. A throwaway prototype, an internal script, or an experiment can be driven with a light touch and reviewed loosely. Code that handles authentication, money, personal data, or safety-critical functions — or any change that is hard to reverse — warrants proportionately closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line review and independent verification.

The normal software development life cycle MUST NOT change when AIs are introduced to automate steps within it. On the contrary, following rigorous development processes, particularly quality control and change management, becomes _more_ important.

== Success criteria

Vibe coding is great for anything that doesn't need to be production-grade, and for research and development projects where there is not a clear end goal. But AI-generated code that is destined for production MUST be validated by a human against its success criteria.

Setting clear success criteria is the key to effective agentic workflows.

Well-defined success criteria ensure that generated code meets your quality standards and project requirements, and models perform best when outcomes are easy to verify. Concrete, executable success criteria — acceptance tests, schemas, linters — matter more for predictable outcomes than the size or capability of the underlying model. Vague guidance leaves output quality to the model's own judgement. Precise, verifiable criteria narrow the gap between a frontier model and a cheaper one, because both are being steered by the same external check rather than by the model's unaided interpretation of the task.

Verification of AI-generated output MUST NOT rely solely on the same AI session that produced it. Wherever a rule or standard can be checked by a machine, it SHOULD be enforced by an independent, deterministic process — a linter, a type-checker, a test suite — run outside the agent's own context, rather than left to the agent's self-assessment. Agents are not reliable judges of their own work. Treat agent-reported success ("tests pass", "this meets the requirement") as a claim to be verified, not a fact.

Directed this way — toward well-defined problems, with clear criteria and rigorous oversight — developers move faster and make better decisions, for instance by using AI to explore more options before committing to one path.

== Software architecture

Another important factor in successful implementation of AI-augmented software development workflows is the quality of the codebase in which the AI agents operate.

A clean, well-structured codebase yields better results from AI code generators, because clear tiers and boundaries constrain the blast radius of any change. Without them, AI tools are more likely to make sweeping changes that have unintended consequences.

Fixing one bug can inadvertently create bugs elsewhere — a time-honored problem in software development that AI tools will magnify if they are not put to work with strong guardrails (enforced at the architectural level) and human oversight (enforced in the software development life cycle).
