= Human-in-the-loop

AI tools amplify engineering skill; they do not substitute for it.

Early academic research suggests that the more skill and experience you have as
a software developer, the better the results you will get from augmenting your
own capabilities with AI tools.

The goal is *AI-augmented software development*. That is, humans directing AI
tools, and remaining firmly in control of the environments in which those tools
operate.

The ultimate objective is NOT to design autonomous replacements for human
decision-making. Taken to this extreme, AI tools are ineffective and, indeed,
outright dangerous.

AI tools — at least not in their 2026 frontier form — cannot substitute for
human decision-making in the critical choices that determine whether a
software system succeeds or fails: judgment about long-term maintainability
and technical debt tradeoffs; understanding of the business domain, unique to
every software system; understanding of how subsystems within that domain
interact across the boundaries of their subdomains; the wisdom to push back on
requirements; context about why certain patterns were chosen in the past;
creativity, and the ability to make unconventional semantic connections; and so
on, and so on.

Where AI tools have proven to be effective is in the removal of more categories
of clerical and repetitive tasks that previously could not be easily automated.

This is the same consequence of every previous wave of programming automation.
High-level languages, compilers, debuggers, IDEs… each automated away another
layer of clerical and repetitive work, freeing us up to spend more time and
thought on the big picture stuff.

No wave of technology disruption — not AI, and not anything that came before it
— moved the ultimate responsibility for a software project's overall scope and
implementation away from humans.

What AI tools do is remove the need to hold low-level detail in your head — the
nuances of a programming language, the boilerplate of a framework — freeing you
to concentrate on higher-level concerns like design, security, maintainability…
ie. problem-solving, the essential core of our discipline.

== Calibrating human involvement

There MUST always be a human-in-the-loop when using AI tools for software
development. Only the _degree_ of human involvement may change from one project
to the next.

The degree to which AI tools are used to augment human capabilities — how much
of the work is handed off to agents, and how tightly that work is supervised —
should be calibrated to the domain and the risk profile of the project.

A throwaway prototype, an internal script, or an experiment can be driven with
a light touch and reviewed loosely. *Vibe coding* is well suited to anything
that doesn't need to be production-grade, and to research and development work
with no clear end goal.

At the other end of the spectrum, code that handles authentication, money,
personal data, or safety-critical functions — or any change that will be hard to
reverse — warrants proportionately closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line
independent verification.

Within those bounds, the degree of supervision is not fixed. As confidence in a
given agentic workflow's reliability grows over time, demonstrated by a track
record of consistent, predictable outcomes, the workflow's human checkpoints
can be deliberately reduced.

None of this displaces the normal software development life cycle. On the
contrary, the more of the work that is delegated to agents, the more rigorous
the development processes, particularly quality control and change management,
need to be.
