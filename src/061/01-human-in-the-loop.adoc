= Human-in-the-loop

AI tools amplify engineering skill; they do not substitute for it. Early academic
research suggests that the more skill and experience you have as a software
developer, the better the results you will get from augmenting your own
capabilities with AI.

Used instead as autonomous replacements for human decision-making, AI tools are
far less effective, and indeed they can be outright dangerous.

That is because what models are weakest at is precisely what decides whether a
system survives: judgment about long-term maintainability and technical debt
tradeoffs; creativity, and the ability to make unconventional semantic
connections; understanding of how systems interact across multiple domains; the
wisdom to push back on requirements that don't make sense; context about why
certain patterns were chosen in the past; and so on.

What AI tools do remove is the need to hold low-level detail in your head — the
nuances of a programming language, the boilerplate of a framework — freeing you
to concentrate on higher-level concerns like design, security, and
problem-solving. That is the same bargain struck by every previous wave of
programming automation — eg. high-level languages, compilers, debuggers, IDEs.
Each automated a layer of clerical and repetitive work, but none moved the
ultimate responsibility for a project's overall scope and implementation away
from humans.

The goal, then, is *AI-augmented software development* — humans directing AI
tools, and firmly in charge of controlling the environments in which those tools
operate. Coding agents are best used to enact human ideas, not as autonomous
coding employees. What changes from one project to the next is not whether humans
are in the loop, but the _degree_ of augmentation — how much of the work is
handed off to agents, and how tightly that work is supervised.

There MUST always be a human-in-the-loop when using AI tools for software
development. Only the _degree_ of human involvement may change, and it MUST
be calibrated to the domain and the risk profile.

A throwaway prototype, an internal script, or an experiment can be driven with
a light touch and reviewed loosely. *Vibe coding* is well suited to anything
that doesn't need to be production-grade, and to research and development work
with no clear end goal.

But code that handles authentication, money, personal data, or safety-critical
functions — or any change that is hard to reverse — warrants proportionately
closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line independent verification.

All AI-generated code destined for production MUST be validated by a human
against its success criteria.

Within those bounds, the degree of supervision is not fixed. As confidence in a
given agentic workflow's reliability grows, demonstrated by a track record of
predictable outcomes, its human checkpoints can be deliberately reduced. This is
the path toward greater autonomy — an incremental transfer of trust, earned by
evidence. Conversely, a workflow that testing shows fails to consistently produce
predictable outcomes is signalling that it needs _more_ human checkpoints, not
fewer.

None of this displaces the normal software development life cycle. On the
contrary, the more of the work that is delegated to agents, the more rigorous
the development processes, particularly quality control and change management,
need to be.
