= Choosing models

When training coding-flavored LLMs, AI companies download millions of examples
of software code from sources like GitHub and use them to train the models.
Subsequently, the companies further optimize the models for coding tasks through
a process known as fine-tuning.

No matter how good that fine-tuning process is, the resulting model will still
be limited by the quality and breadth of its training data. If the training data
is biased toward certain languages, frameworks, or programming styles, the model
will reflect those biases in its outputs.

This limitation is true of all AI models — not only coding agents — that are
based on the
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_(deep_learning)[transformer
architecture]. It's because a model's knowledge of semantic associations is
learned from its training data. In neural networks, words act as addresses that
unlock semantic relationships encoded in the model's parameters. Context shapes
meaning. Thus, the word "bank" means something different when preceded by
"river" or "central". Larger LLMs can potentially make deeper contextual
connections than smaller ones, because they have more parameters linked in more
multidimensional ways, producing a richer map of semantic relationships.

The effect is that today's AI coding agents are very good at generating code in
modern programming languages like JavaScript and HTML, but they are generally
poor at things like 6502 Assembly and niche domain-specific languages. They just
don't have the deep knowledge of those languages — the depth of semantic
associations encoded in their parameters — gleaned from their training data to
produce good outputs.

This is a key consideration when choosing a model to use for a given task. You
want to choose a model that has been trained on data relevant to the task at
hand. General purpose models will be adequate for many tasks in modern
programming languages, but for more specialized tasks you may get better results
using more specialized models.

== Capability tiers

Models are commonly grouped into tiers that trade capability against speed and
cost:

* *Frontier* models are the current state of the art from well-resourced labs
(eg. Anthropic's Claude Opus, OpenAI's GPT flagship, Google's Gemini Pro). They
are the most capable but also the slowest and most expensive, so they SHOULD be
reserved for work where their capability changes the outcome — planning,
architecture, complex problem-solving, and security analysis. See the sections
on AI-assisted and agentic workflows for where frontier models fit in a tiered
workflow, and on cost optimization for the economics of this choice.

* *Mid-tier* models (eg. Claude Sonnet, GPT mid-tier releases, open-weight
workhorses like Llama 70B or Qwen) balance capability, speed, and cost, and are
adequate for most day-to-day tasks, including a large share of coding work.

* *Light* models (eg. Claude Haiku, Gemini Flash, GPT mini) trade capability for
speed and cost. They suit high-volume, latency-sensitive, or mechanical work —
classification, extraction, routing, simple lookups.

* *Reasoning* models spend extra compute on an internal chain-of-thought before
answering, at the cost of latency and token spend. Use them for problems with a
verifiable chain of logic — maths, planning, multi-step analysis, complex
debugging. See the section on tuning model behavior for how to scale the
reasoning/thinking budget to the task, rather than defaulting it to maximum.

* *Specialist* models are fine-tuned for a narrow domain rather than
general-purpose use (eg. a search-grounded model tuned for factual retrieval
with citations, or a code-completion model tuned for fill-in-the-middle IDE
autocomplete). Prefer a specialist model over a general-purpose one where the
task matches its specialization closely.

* *Open-weight* models have publicly released weights that can be downloaded and
run on your own hardware. They typically trail frontier models in capability by
some months, but eliminate per-token cost and keep data on infrastructure you
control. They are RECOMMENDED for privacy-sensitive or offline work, and for
high-volume tasks where local hardware is adequate to the job.

Matching the model tier to the task, rather than defaulting to the biggest model
for everything or the cheapest for everything, is the central discipline. See
the section on cost optimization for the economic case, and the section on
agentic workflows for how this discipline extends to multi-agent, tiered
architectures (frontier models as supervisors, cheaper models as sub-agents
executing pre-approved plans).

== Matching tasks to models

Different task types stress different model capabilities — language quality,
step-by-step reasoning, factual grounding, or raw speed — and the capability a
task demands matters more than a model's headline benchmark scores. For example,
proofreading is linguistically demanding but reasoning-light, whereas a maths
word problem is the reverse; a strong general-purpose model at low reasoning
effort suits the former, a reasoning model at high effort the latter.

When selecting a model for a task, first identify the dominant capability that
task demands, then choose the cheapest tier and capability type that satisfies
it. Reserve frontier and reasoning models for tasks where that capability is
genuinely the bottleneck.

[NOTE]
======
Some tasks are not a matter of choosing a bigger or more capable model at all.
No model reliably beats a deterministic tool at exact computation, so the right
choice is not a model but a script. See the section on agentic versus automated
workflows.
======

== Benchmarks and leaderboards

Public benchmarks and leaderboards (eg. SWE-bench for coding, Terminal-Bench for
agentic terminal tasks, LiveBench for contamination-resistant general
capability) are a useful first filter when comparing candidate models, and
cross-provider aggregators (eg. Artificial Analysis, OpenRouter rankings,
LMArena) are useful for comparing capability, speed, and price across labs.

Public benchmarks are no substitute for evaluating candidate models against your
own representative tasks. See the section on evaluation for how to build and run
your own evals when choosing between models.
