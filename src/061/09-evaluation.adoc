= Evaluation

Evals (evaluations) are structured tests that measure how well an LLM, agent, skill, prompt, or AI-assisted workflow performs on a defined set of tasks. They are the AI equivalent of unit and regression tests, and they are widely cited as the most important – yet most often skipped – practice in building reliable AI systems.

In AI-assisted development, evals answer a question you can otherwise only guess at: did a change to your model, prompt, skill, or harness actually improve outcomes, or quietly regress them? Without evals, every such change is made blind. This chapter sets out evals as a general discipline; for the specific case of evaluating skills, see the detailed treatment in the reusable context chapter.

== What to evaluate

Within the scope of this standard – using AI to build software – evals apply to the components of your AI setup, rather than to an end-user product:

* *Model selection.* Public benchmarks and leaderboards (see the choosing models chapter) are a useful first filter, but they MUST NOT be treated as a substitute for evaluating candidate models on your own representative tasks.
* *Prompts, skills, and rules.* See the detailed treatment in the reusable context chapter.
* *Agents and harness configuration.* Whether a change to tools, guardrails, or model tiering helps or harms.
* *Generated outputs.* The code, designs, or documentation the AI produces, judged against your acceptance criteria.

== The delta is the point

The central mechanism of evaluation is comparison. Run the same eval in two configurations – with and without a change, or against the previous version – and look at the *delta* in pass rate, latency, and token cost. This tells you what the change buys against what it costs. A change that does not move the pass rate is not earning its keep, whatever its other appeal. (This is the same principle applied to cost in the cost optimization chapter and to skills in the reusable context chapter.)

== Building an eval suite

A test case has three parts: a realistic input, a description of what success looks like, and any fixtures it needs. Eval suites SHOULD live in version control alongside the code or prompts they exercise, so the two evolve together.

Start with a small suite covering your most common and most failure-prone cases, and grow it from real failures rather than speculation. Beyond a handful of cases, it is RECOMMENDED to use an established eval framework – such as https://github.com/openai/evals[OpenAI Evals], which pairs a runner with an open registry of reusable evals – rather than maintaining ad-hoc scripts.

== Assertions, judges, and human review

Match the checking method to what is being measured:

* Use objective, evidence-backed *assertions* for properties that can be verified mechanically (a regex matches, a file exists, a test passes).
* Use an *LLM-as-judge* – ideally a blind comparison of two outputs – for qualitative properties such as clarity, tone, or organisation that resist mechanical checks.
* Reserve *human review* for what neither of the above captures.

Do not over-specify assertions up front; observe what the system actually produces, then write checks against those outputs. Account for non-determinism – run several samples rather than asserting a single exact output (see the tuning model behavior chapter).

== When to run evals

Evals SHOULD be run:

* before adopting a new model or model version;
* before changing prompts, skills, rules, or harness configuration;
* as a regression gate in CI for any automated step that depends on an AI component.

As a baseline: any AI-assisted step whose output quality materially affects the delivered software SHOULD be backed by evals before it is relied upon in a routine workflow.
