= Technical Standards

This is a personal collection of best practices and guidelines for software
design and construction.

Technical standards define what "good" looks like. They describe the desirable
end state of working software, and of the artifacts that support it — from
requirements specifications and design docs, to automated tests and
deployment pipelines.

They are written for a technical audience: software engineers, architects, and
technical leads. Business stakeholders have their own means of stating what
they need from a system, through product requirements documents and service-level
agreements. Technical standards are what the people who design, build, and
operate a system hold each other to.

Technical standards have numerous benefits. They shorten onboarding, reduce
the number of decisions that must be revisited on every project, and give
reviewers a shared, objective basis for feedback. But their main value lies in
containing technical sprawl across an organization.

To be useful and enduring, technical standards must strike a balance between the
abstract and the concrete. Abstract design principles are the most enduring but
offer nothing to verify against. At the other extreme, if technical standards
are treated as requirements specifications, eg. pinning numeric targets for
metrics like service availability and latency, they become coupled to a
particular project at a point in time. Technical standards define outcomes that
are just concrete enough that a system can be verified against them.

Technical standard specify outcomes for technical quality, but they do not
prescribe methods and tools for achieving those outcomes. Topics like iterative
and incremental development, feedback loops, and ways of working are therefore
out of scope.

[NOTE]
======
You are welcome to fork and adapt these technical standards for your own needs.
The https://github.com/kieranpotts/standards[source files are on GitHub].
======

== Requirements levels

Throughout these technical standards, the capitalized words REQUIRED, MUST,
MUST NOT, RECOMMENDED, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT, OPTIONAL, and MAY are to be
interpreted as described in https://www.ietf.org/rfc/rfc2119.txt[IETF RFC 2119].
