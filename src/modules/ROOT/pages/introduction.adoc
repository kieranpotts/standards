= Introduction to technical standards

A technical standard defines what "`good`" looks like from a technical
perspective. It is a durable statement of the end state a system should reach,
written down so that it does not have to be re-argued on every project, in
every code review, by every engineer who joins.

This page describes what technical standards are for, what belongs in one and
what does not, and how they are meant to be applied.

== Scope

The scope of technical standards is broad, covering all aspects of the software
development lifecycle: requirements specification, system design, coding
conventions, testing, deployment, and monitoring. Anything that has a
recognizably better and worse answer, and where the better answer holds across
projects, is a candidate for being made a technical standard.

The focus of a technical standard is the desirable end state, not the means of
getting there. Methods and tools that help a team reach that end state —
iterative and incremental development, feedback loops, ways of splitting work,
ceremonies for coordinating it — are a separate concern. Technical standards
should outlive any team and project, and not be constrained to any particular
development process.

A standard is most useful when it is written as a concrete, verifiable end
state. Abstract principles of programming and software design — coupling
and cohesion, the merits of one paradigm over another, the philosophy of
abstraction — are the reasoning behind standards rather than standards
themselves. "`Prefer low coupling`" is a principle, not a standard.

Numeric targets for service availability, time to recovery, request latency,
and the like are particular to one application, driven by what that application
is for and who depends on it. They belong in that system's requirements
specification, not in its technical standards.

== Why standardize

Standards convert a recurring decision into a settled one. The value is
cumulative rather than dramatic: no single ruling matters much, but the absence
of all of them means every project re-litigates the same ground, and arrives at
a different answer each time.

* *Decision cost.* Most technical decisions are not close calls, and spending
  fresh deliberation on them is waste. A standard is a cached decision, with
  the reasoning attached for the cases where the cache should be invalidated.

* *Consistency across systems.* Systems built to the same standards can be
  read, operated, and maintained by the same people. This compounds: the
  tenth system built to a familiar shape costs far less to take on than the
  first.

* *Containing technical sprawl.* In enterprise contexts, including a
  technology in a technical standard signifies that it is approved for use in
  development, testing, and production. That approval is a boundary, and it
  matters most in large-scale distributed systems, where an unchecked spread
  of languages, datastores, and deployment models becomes an operational
  liability long before anyone decides it has.

* *Onboarding.* A written standard is the fastest available answer to "`how do
  we do this here?`", and it does not depend on catching the right person at
  the right moment.

== Ownership

Technical standards are owned by the technical stakeholders in a project —
the engineers, architects, and technical leads who will live with the
consequences. Standards imposed from outside that group tend to be complied
with rather than believed, which is a distinction that shows up quickly under
schedule pressure.

Ownership implies maintenance. A standard nobody is responsible for is a
standard nobody updates, and an unmaintained standard decays into an obstacle:
it describes a world that no longer exists, so practitioners route around it,
and the habit of routing around one standard generalizes to the rest.

== Writing a technical standard

A good standard is:

* *Concrete.* It describes an observable end state. A reader should be able to
  look at a system and say whether it conforms.

* *Justified.* It records why, briefly. The reasoning is what lets a future
  reader tell a rule that still applies from one that was a workaround for a
  problem since fixed.

* *Scoped.* It says what it covers and, where the boundary is not obvious,
  what it does not. Standards that quietly expand their remit start to
  contradict each other.

* *Graded.* Not every requirement carries equal weight. Requirement levels —
  MUST, SHOULD, MAY, and their negations, as defined in
  https://www.ietf.org/rfc/rfc2119.txt[IETF RFC 2119] — distinguish a hard
  constraint from a strong default, and a body of standards that treats
  everything as mandatory will be ignored wholesale.

* *Current.* Technologies are superseded, tooling matures, and yesterday's
  hard-won best practice becomes today's liability. Standards are living
  documents, and revision is the normal state rather than a sign of an earlier
  mistake.

== Applying standards

Nothing in a body of technical standards is meant as a rigid rule. Standards
frame day-to-day decisions; they do not settle them in advance. Software
engineering is the balancing of competing requirements, and it is often
necessary to bend a default constraint to accommodate a particular one. No
standard is perfect, and none is applicable to every situation.

The golden rule is this: when you go against a standard, document it. Every
standard can be broken, except that one.

A deviation MUST record why the standard was broken, the implications of
breaking it, and the conditions that must hold before it can be re-applied.
This applies at every level of abstraction, from a single line of code to a
coarse-grained design decision.

[NOTE]
======
The requirement to document deviations is what keeps a standard honest. A
deviation that is recorded is evidence — and a standard that accumulates
enough of them is telling you it is wrong, not that its readers are
undisciplined.
======
