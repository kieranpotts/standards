= AI use cases

.Key requirements
****
* Correctness MUST be established by a human or a deterministic check.
* Output MUST NOT be trusted on the model's confidence alone.
* Agentic checks MUST NOT replace deterministic ones.
* A model MUST NOT make the decisions that belong to a human.
****

== Inherent properties of LLMs

Before we can decide where AI tools will be best placed to enhance software delivery, we must first appreciate the fundamental characteristics of large language models that distinguish them from classical forms of computational automation.

First, LLMs run on tokens, not facts. A token is a fragment of a word or image, and it is the only unit a model reads and writes. Training a model involves setting the model's *weights*, which encode semantic relationships between tokens, capturing which tokens tend to go together in which contexts.

A model is not taught facts, and it does not store text or knowledge. Rather, factual output is a matter of probability. Where the training data is accurate and plentiful, the most probable continuation of a sequence of tokens will often happen to be factually correct. But where the training data is thin, contradictory, or plain wrong, models are more likely to produce output that is factually incorrect. Whether a model's output is correct or not is simply a probabilistic outcome determined by the quality of the original training data from which the model's weights were formulated.

A large language model does not "know" anything. You can see this for yourself. Ask a model directly — outside of a harness that may ground the responses in retrieved facts — to quote a famous passage from memory, and it will often get at least some of the words wrong. It will reconstruct the general shape of the quotation, rather than reproducing it perfectly. Ask repeatedly for a random number and watch how often 42 is returned. A model merely emits the tokens its training data made most probable in that context, and the culture its training data is drawn from has made 42 the most probable "random" number of all, thanks to Douglas Adams.

Models produce output that is convincing in its assertions, but it is merely a probabilistic guess at which tokens should come next, in light of statistical patterns in the training data and the context of the current conversation. A model's aim is only to produce text that is coherent and plausible, and that fits the context.

Models have no inherent notion of truth. Training can improve how well a model flags its own uncertainty and doubt, but this can never be depended upon. Models will always be prone to emitting fabrications as fluently as facts. A model's confidence in its output is no guide to the correctness of that output.

Worse, models are impressionable. They will take on whatever role and stance is implied by the context, and they lean toward giving agreeable answers in a sycophantic tone. Model output can even be steered by framing the input text, for example by using flattery, urgency, or the promise of a reward. That's because every token in the context shifts the probabilities of which tokens come next.

AI models can be surprisingly creative. What a model generates is not confined to solutions it has seen before, and it will sometimes reach for a novel solution that a human would not have tried. Dialing up a model's "temperature" setting increases the randomness of its output, yielding more innovative results. The trade-off is reduced predictability and correctness of output.

Output quality also deteriorates as the context grows. The longer an xref:garden::agent.adoc[*agent session*] runs and the fuller its context gets, the more likely the model is to lose track of an earlier instruction. Models will even start to contradict themselves if a conversation is left to run long enough. This phenomenon is known as xref:garden::context-rot.adoc[*context rot*].

Finally, it is important to appreciate that a large language model is not capable of explaining its own reasoning. If you ask a model to explain how it arrived at a particular output, it will just make stuff up. The explanation will simply be derived in the normal way — by predicting the next sequence of tokens based on the prior context. It is not true introspection as we humans understand it.

These properties of large language models converge on a single, defining failure mode. An xref:garden::hallucination.adoc[*hallucination*] is model output that is fluent and plausible but false. Hallucinations take the form of fabricated facts, non-existent APIs, invented citations, made-up introspection, and other confident-sounding answers to questions the model cannot actually answer.

== When to use AI

Hallucinations are a feature, not a bug, of large language models. Creativity in output is precisely what sets LLMs apart from classic systems of computation. It is this property that gives LLMs an entirely different set of use cases from traditional software, letting us automate whole categories of work that we previously could not automate, because that work cannot be reduced to fixed rules and logic.

Large language models are nothing like traditional software. Traditional software is deterministic and authoritative. Given the same input, traditional software returns the same output, and that output is either correct or a bug. A language model is non-deterministic. The same input can yield a different result on every run.

Models work by probabilistic inference over patterns in language, code, and structure. They do not work by following fixed rules and logic. That is precisely their power. Thus, the strongest use cases for AI tools in software delivery are wherever a task can be framed as recognizing, completing, transforming, or judging patterns. There are a few broad categories of work that fit this criterion.

* *Translation.* Converting a structure from one representation to another, eg. a schema into types, an API response into a client, prose into markup, one language or framework's idioms into another's — anything where the mapping cannot be purely mechanical, because there's too much irregularity to reduce the translation to a set of fixed rules.

* *Summarizing and explaining.* Compressing a large body of code or text into its essentials, explaining an unfamiliar module, or surfacing what a change does.

* *Exploring options.* Enumerating several approaches to a problem, with the trade-offs between them made explicit, as raw material for a human decision.

* *Inferential quality checks.* Judgment-based checks that no deterministic tool can perform, eg. reviewing against intent, interrogating requirements, or detecting drift from a specification.

* *Drafting.* Producing a first version — eg. of a function, a test, a commit message, a docstring, a design sketch — that a human then completes. Getting past the blank page is often the most expensive part of creative work.

Large language models also make for great *thinking companions*. Because they reason from patterns across an enormous body of language and code, rather than from lived experience of your problem, models do not share your assumptions, your blind spots, or your attachment to the approach you have already committed to. They can offer a fresh perspective to sharpen your own judgment. They can help you to enumerate alternatives, stress-test a design, or argue an opposing case.

Taken further, models can be engines of *innovation*. Innovation is mostly the product of connecting ideas that were not related to begin with, and a language model is, quite literally, a connection-making machine. Training encodes associations between tokens that no human would think to draw explicitly. Ask a person to list unconventional uses for a paper clip and they will produce a dozen or so in two minutes. A capable model will produce many more, and faster. The loose boundary between real and fabricated associations that causes hallucination is the same property that makes these novel connections possible.

== When not to use AI

Playing to a model's strengths also means recognizing the tasks for which an AI tool is the wrong instrument, and a deterministic one would usually be better.

* *When a deterministic tool already does the job.* Anything that can be checked mechanically, eg. syntax, types, schemas, formatting, or behavior under test, SHOULD be handled by the deterministic tool built for it. Those tools are faster, cheaper, and exact, and they do not hallucinate. Using a language model where a compiler, a linter, a formatter, or a codemod would serve is slower, more expensive, and less reliable. Agentic checks add to deterministic ones. They MUST NOT replace them.

* *When you cannot verify the output.* If neither you nor an engineered check can tell whether the result is correct, the model's fluency becomes a liability. A confident, plausible, wrong answer is worse than an obvious gap. Do not delegate work whose correctness you have no means of establishing. This applies especially in a domain you do not understand well enough to catch the errors yourself.

* *When the decision belongs to a human.* Tacit knowledge, judgment, and accountability cannot be delegated. Deciding what is worth building, weighing long-term trade-offs, pushing back on a requirement, and answering for the outcome remain human responsibilities. A model may inform these decisions. It MUST NOT make them.

* *When the stakes are high and hard to reverse.* Actions that are irreversible or that have a large blast radius warrant proportionately more human scrutiny. These include changes touching authentication, money, personal data, safety-critical functions, or production infrastructure. The harder a mistake is to undo, the weaker the case for handing the task to a tool whose output you would have to check line by line anyway.

* *When novelty exceeds the model's experience.* Frontier models are strongest on patterns they have seen many times. Novel problems sit outside that comfort zone, eg. a design with no close precedent, reasoning specific to your business domain, or a subtle interaction across subsystem boundaries. The model will still answer confidently. Its confidence is not evidence.

* *When it would erode skill that must be kept.* Delegating the fundamentals you are still learning, or that your team must retain to supervise the tools at all, trades a short-term gain for a long-term loss. Judgment and taste are built by doing the work. A team that has outsourced all the underlying work loses its ability to tell good output from bad.

* *When data must not leave your control.* Sending code or data to a third-party model may breach confidentiality, licensing, or data-protection obligations. Where it does, that constraint decides the matter, regardless of how well the task would otherwise fit. The exception is a workflow that runs on a model and infrastructure cleared for the data in question.

An AI tool is a force multiplier where it fits, and a multiplier of cost and risk where it does not.

// === Where the technology shines

// The same properties that make a model a poor substitute for deterministic software make it good at work that was once the preserve of humans. A language model is strong where the task is linguistic, open-ended, or a matter of judgment: writing and rewriting prose, analyzing and summarizing material, inventing options, making an educated guess under uncertainty, holding a conversation, and taking on a role or persona on demand. It is unreliable at what traditional software does effortlessly, which is automating a repeatable process with exact precision or carrying out a complex computation the same way every time. A model can be used for such work, but it cannot be _relied on_ for it. This is a new class of capability, not a more flexible scripting engine. Reach for a script, not a model, wherever the task is exact and repeatable.

// == Beyond code generation

// Code generation is the most visible application of AI to software development, and it attracts most of the attention. But it may not be the most valuable use case for AI tools in software development. Greater returns may be had from applying AI to the work that surrounds code generation.

// Before AI, the only quality checks that could be automated were those reducible to deterministic rules: static analysis, type checking, schema validation, and the execution of behavioral tests.

// Everything else required inference: judging whether an abstraction earns its keep; noticing that a design document no longer describes the system it documents; spotting that a change has widened the attack surface; or asking whether a specification describes what the customer needs.

// These checks turn on judgment, opinion, and knowledge of context, so until recently only humans could perform them. And because humans are expensive, they were performed rarely — at a design review, a pre-release threat modeling workshop, or an annual architecture audit — if at all.

// Frontier models, given good guides, do a lot of this work well. That changes the economics. Inferential checks that were affordable only occasionally can now run on every major change. Agents can be configured to.

// * *Review code changes* against the project's standards, conventions, and architectural intent — not merely against its linting rules.

// * *Run threat modeling workshops* on a far more regular cadence than a human-only team can sustain.

// * *Detect drift* between the evolving implementation and the design documents, keeping the architectural artifacts synchronized with reality.

// * *Interrogate requirements* for ambiguity, unstated assumptions, and missing acceptance criteria, before any code is written.

// * *Propose architectures* from requirements specifications, with the options considered and the trade-offs between them made explicit.

// * *Decompose designs* into small, incremental units of delivery, feeding backlogs and supporting continuous integration.

// * *Audit a codebase* for structural drift — shallow abstractions, tangled dependencies, repeated patterns — and report a prioritized set of remedies.

// * *Triage incoming issues*, reproducing reported defects and classifying them ahead of human review.

// * *Validate delivered software against users' actual needs*, rather than only against the acceptance criteria agreed up front.

// The common thread is that none of these are code generation. Each is a quality control activity that, until recently, only a human could perform.

// The gain in offloading this work to agents is usually not that agents do it better — often, they will not. The gain is that the work happens at all, that it happens earlier in the development lifecycle, and on a much higher cadence.

// Some of the items above are exceptions, and worth dwelling on: reviewing code changes and detecting drift. Both are, at their core, pattern-matching problems — holding a change up against a large body of standards, conventions, and architectural intent, or holding an implementation up against the design it is meant to embody, and flagging wherever the two diverge. This is precisely the work a model does faster and more tirelessly than a human. Across enough volume, its speed at pattern recognition can match or outrun a careful human reviewer, rather than merely relieving them of the labor.

// Here the model's non-determinism — a liability wherever there is a single right answer — becomes an asset. Each pass is a fresh analysis: run the same review twice and it returns a different, overlapping set of findings. Rather than suppress that variance, exploit it. Run the review more than once, or across more than one model, and take the union of what they surface. A single human reviewer offers no comparable breadth across repeated passes. This is the same property the evaluation practice relies on when it samples a model several times to characterize its behavior.

// The same reasoning extends to a use that surprises many: the more creative forms of security testing, penetration testing chief among them. A pen test is not a fixed checklist run to completion. It is open-ended, adversarial exploration — guessing where a system might give way and inventing inputs to probe it. That is pattern-matching against a vast corpus of known weaknesses and exploit techniques, and precisely the kind of divergent, breadth-first search at which a model excels. Its non-determinism is again the asset: each run reaches for a different attack path, so repeated and parallel runs cover more ground than a single deterministic sweep ever could. The same holds, more broadly, for design and architecture work, where enumerating unconventional approaches matters more than converging on one. None of this removes the human — findings still need a practitioner to confirm, prioritize, and act on — but the model is a genuinely capable partner in the exploratory work, not merely a labor-saver.

// Because hallucination is intrinsic, it cannot be trusted away — only checked away. Output whose correctness cannot be established by a human or an engineered check MUST NOT be trusted on the model's confidence alone, and a confident, plausible, wrong answer is more dangerous than an obvious gap precisely because nothing in its surface marks it as wrong. This is the root reason agentic workflows depend on sensors: verification exists because the model cannot self-certify its output. The techniques that raise the truth rate — grounding the model in real sources, prompting well, cross-checking against a second context — reduce how often hallucination bites. None of them removes it, because none of them changes what the model fundamentally is.

// One consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to underestimate until you have been caught by it: a model rarely answers "I don't know" unprompted. Training has made models better at expressing uncertainty and asking clarifying questions, but faced with a question it has no basis to answer, a model will often still not fall silent or flag its own uncertainty — it emits the most probable continuation regardless, with the same fluency and the same confident register it uses for everything else. There is no dependable internal signal that distinguishes "I am recalling a fact" from "I am filling a gap plausibly", so nothing surfaces to warn you when the second is what happened. Treat any output that reads like confident recall — a specific date, a citation, an API signature, a quoted figure — as the case most worth independent verification, precisely because its surface gives no hint of which case you are in. Direct recall of a specific, checkable fact is one of the weakest things to ask a model for at all. A traditional search engine or an authoritative source will serve better, precisely because it can say nothing at all rather than confabulate.

// This connects to a design trade-off worth understanding. A model that sticks closely to the statistical patterns in its training data reproduces what it has seen faithfully, but handles inputs unlike anything in that data poorly, producing output that is stale, generic, or subtly wrong. (Overfitting, a failure to generalize beyond the training data, is a training-time defect, and it is addressed in training.) At inference time, sampling randomness (ie. temperature) controls how much the output varies. Introducing controlled unpredictability means the model does not always default to the single most probable, most "seen-before" continuation. That trade-off runs in both directions: the same randomness that produces fresh, varied output also widens the opening for hallucination, because it makes the model more willing to wander from the token sequence the training data most strongly supports. There is no setting that removes the trade-off, only ways of choosing where to sit on it for a given task.

// Hallucination also originates upstream, in the training data itself, which is why a substantial share of frontier model development effort now goes into curating that data — sifting reliable sources from unreliable ones, and opinion, satire, and fiction from statements of fact, so the patterns a model absorbs are less often patterns of confident falsehood. Combined with post-training techniques and, in some models, an explicit "reflect and revise" step that lets a model reconsider part of its own output before returning it, measured hallucination rates have been falling release over release. None of this changes the underlying mechanism described above, and none of it justifies expecting the problem to be solved outright — a lower rate is not the absence of the failure mode, and the discipline of verification does not relax as the rate improves.

// Taken together this is the *AI paradox*: the same mechanism that makes a model prone to hallucinate — generating a plausible continuation with no anchor to whether the training data supports it — is what lets it reach genuinely novel connections that lie outside anything it was explicitly trained to do. Unreliability and creative reach are not two separate properties in tension. They are the same property, read in the two different lights of failure and capability. This is why the discipline this standard asks for is not to suppress the mechanism — that would suppress the capability along with the flaw — but to route each kind of task to where the mechanism helps rather than hurts: away from precise recall and mission-critical, error-intolerant work, and toward the open-ended, judgment-shaped, or genuinely novel work developed next.
