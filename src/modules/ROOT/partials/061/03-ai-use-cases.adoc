= AI use cases

.Key requirements
****
* Output MUST NOT be trusted on the model's confidence alone.
* Correctness MUST be established by a human or a deterministic check.
* Agentic checks MUST NOT replace deterministic ones.
* A model MUST NOT make the decisions that belong to a human.
* Irreversible or large-blast-radius actions MUST receive proportionately more human scrutiny.
* Data MUST NOT be sent to a model that is not cleared to receive it.
****

== Inherent properties of LLMs

Before we can decide where AI tools will be best placed to enhance software delivery, we must first appreciate the fundamental characteristics of large language models that distinguish them from classical forms of automation.

First, LLMs run on tokens, not facts. A token is a fragment of a word or image, and it is the only unit a model reads and writes. Training a model involves setting the model's *weights*, which encode semantic relationships between tokens, capturing which tokens tend to go together in which contexts.

A model is not taught facts, and it does not store text or knowledge. Rather, factual output is a matter of probability. Where the training data is accurate and plentiful, the most probable continuation of a sequence of tokens will often happen to form text that is factually correct. But where the training data is thin, contradictory, or plain wrong, models are more likely to produce output that is similarly wrong.

A large language model does not "know" anything. You can see this for yourself. Ask a model directly (outside of a harness) to quote a famous passage from memory, and it will often get at least some of the words wrong. It will reconstruct the general shape of the quotation, rather than reproducing it perfectly. Or, ask repeatedly for a random number and watch how often 42 is returned. Training data has made 42 the most probably "random" number of all, thanks for Douglas Adams.

Models have no inherent notion of truth. This property manifests as their most well-known failure mode: xref:garden::hallucination.adoc[*hallucination*]. An hallucination is model output that is fluent and plausible, but false. Hallucinations take the form of fabricated facts, invented citations, made-up introspection, and non-existent APIs. It happens because a model's aim is only to produce text that is coherent and plausible, and that fits the context. Training can improve how well a model flags its own uncertainty and doubt, but this can never be depended upon. Models will always be prone to emitting fabrications as fluently as facts. A model's confidence in its output is no guide to the correctness of that output.

Worse, models are impressionable. They will take on whatever role and stance is implied by the context, and they lean toward giving agreeable answers in a sycophantic tone. Model output can even be steered by framing the input text, for example by using flattery, urgency, or the promise of a reward. That's because every token in the context shifts the probabilities of which tokens come next.

This agreeableness has the potential to become an echo chamber. Without good prompt engineering, a model will reflect your own assumptions and viewpoints back at you, confirmed and elaborated upon. The result is easily mistaken for an independent opinion.

Models also carry the worldview of their training data. Most xref:garden::large-language-model.adoc[*pre-training*] data is drawn from the open web, which over-represents English-language and Western sources, and post-training is shaped by a comparatively narrow group of practitioners. A model's default assumptions, values, and frames of reference reflect those origins, and studies have repeatedly shown that language models can amplify stereotypes about race, gender, and other characteristics. Better-curated training data is making these biases less overt over time. But the subtler that the inherent biases become, the less likely they are to be noticeable in the output.

AI models can be surprisingly creative. What a model generates is not confined to solutions it has seen before, and it will sometimes reach for a novel solution that a human would not have tried. Dialing up a model's *temperature* setting increases the randomness of its output, yielding more innovative results. The trade-off is reduced predictability and correctness of output.

Output quality also deteriorates as the context grows. The longer an xref:garden::agent.adoc[*agent session*] runs and the fuller its context gets, the more likely the model is to lose track of an earlier instruction. Models will even start to contradict themselves if a conversation is left to run long enough. This phenomenon is known as xref:garden::context-rot.adoc[*context rot*].

Finally, a large language model is not capable of explaining its own reasoning. If you ask a model to explain how it arrived at a particular output, it will just make stuff up. The explanation will simply be derived in the normal way, by predicting the next sequence of tokens based on the prior context. It is not true introspection as we humans understand it.

== When to use AI

Hallucinations and all the other properties of LLMs are features, not bugs. Creativity in their output is precisely what sets LLMs apart from classical systems of computation. It is this property that gives LLMs an entirely different set of use cases from traditional software. It lets us automate whole categories of work that we previously could not automate, because that work cannot be reduced to fixed rules and logic.

Traditional software is deterministic and authoritative. Given the same input, it returns the same output, and that output is either correct or a bug. A language model is non-deterministic. The same input can yield a different result on every run.

Models work by probabilistic inference over patterns in language, code, and structure, not by following fixed rules and logic. It follows that the strongest use cases for AI tools in software delivery are wherever a task can be framed as creating, completing, recognizing, transforming, or judging patterns. There are a few broad categories of work that fit this criterion.

* *Code generation* is the most visible application of AI to software development, and it attracts most of the attention. LLMs are particularly good at producing a first version — eg. of a function, a test, a commit message, a docstring, a design sketch — that a human then reviews and completes. This is valuable. Getting past the blank page is often the most expensive part of creative work.

* *Inferential quality checks.* Beyond code generation, the review and analysis of code may be one of the most valuable applications of AI tools in software delivery. LLMs can be highly effective at taking on judgment-based quality checks that no deterministic tool can perform, eg. reviewing a change against the project's conventions and architectural intent, interrogating requirements for ambiguity and unstated assumptions, detecting drift between an implementation and its design documents, or auditing a codebase for structural decay. Whereas we've had code generation tools for a long time, much of the quality control work on generated code could previously be done only by humans, and that work is enormously labor intensive. We can now delegate some of this to AI tools.

* *Translation.* LLMs are good at converting a structure from one representation to another, eg. a schema into types, an API response into a client, prose into markup, or one language or framework's idioms into another's — anything where the mapping cannot be purely mechanical, because there's too much irregularity to reduce the translation to a set of fixed rules.

* *Summarizing and explaining.* LLMs are very effective at compress a large body of code or text into its essentials. This is more valuable than it sounds, because so much of a developer's time goes on reading and research: getting oriented in an unfamiliar codebase, working out what a long pull request actually changes, or wading through a sprawling specification or incident thread to find the bits that matter. A model can do that first pass in seconds, saving hours of effort across a team on a daily basis.

* *Exploring options.* LLMs can enumerate several approaches to a problem, with the trade-offs between them made explicit, as raw material for a human decision. This can yield better final decisions, because a wider range of design options can be considered, and in greater detail. Models have no attachment to past design choices, and are less likely to dismiss unconventional approaches.

* *Red teaming.* Threat modeling and penetration testing are open-ended, creative pursuits that depend on looking at a system from an attacker's angle and following hunches wherever they lead. LLMs can play the attacker at a scale no person can match. A model does not tire or lose interest, and it can hold an entire codebase in view at once, so it can easily trace untrusted input across every module and trust boundary. That property makes LLMs highly effective at surfacing inconsistent validation, missing authorization checks, and other weaknesses that a human reviewer would need months of familiarity with the code to notice. This work can now be done much more frequently than it has traditionally — even continuously, plugged into CI/CD pipelines, significantly improving the security posture of deployments.

AI tools also remove clerical and repetitive work that previously resisted automation. This is a continuation of earlier waves of programming automation. Compilers, high-level languages, debuggers, and IDEs each automated away another layer of clerical work in software construction. AI tools go further, removing the need to remember the low-level details of programming syntax and the APIs of application frameworks and libraries, freeing us to focus on higher-level concerns like design, security, maintainability, and problem-solving.

Using AIs as *thinking companions* is probably the most prevalent use of AI tools today. A model can be prompted not to share your assumptions, your blind spots, or your attachment to an approach you've already committed to, so it offers a fresh perspective, eg. stress-testing a design or arguing an opposing case for an approach.

Taken further, models can be used as engines of *innovation*. Innovation is mostly a matter of connecting previously unrelated ideas, and a language model is, quite literally, a connection-making machine. Training encodes associations between tokens that no human would think to draw explicitly. Ask a person for unconventional uses for a paper clip and they will produce a dozen or so in two minutes. A capable model will produce many more, faster.

In all of these uses, AI tools *amplify* engineering skill. They do not substitute for it. Power tools did not eliminate carpenters, and spreadsheets did not eliminate accountants. They changed what the job required, and what being "skilled" meant within those domains. xref:garden::ai-coding-assistant.adoc[*Coding agents*] are the same kind of shift. The tasks that make up a software developer's day change, sometimes fundamentally, but the underlying job remains the same: understanding a problem, deciding what to build, and verifying the solution.

Early academic research into this field suggests that the more skill and experience you have as a software developer, the better the results you will get from AI tools. AI augmentation raises the floor for less experienced developers, too. But it appears that, rather than leveling the playing field, AI tools widen the gap between the most and least capable developers.

== When not to use AI

Playing to a model's strengths also means recognizing the tasks for which an AI tool is the wrong instrument, and a deterministic one would be better. An AI tool is a force multiplier where it fits, and a multiplier of cost and risk where it does not.

Anything that can be checked mechanically, eg. syntax, types, schemas, formatting, or behavior-under-test, SHOULD be handled by the deterministic tool built for it. A compiler, a linter, a formatter, or a codemod is faster, cheaper, and exact, and it does not hallucinate. Agentic checks can augment deterministic ones, but they MUST NOT replace them.

Precise recall of a specific, checkable fact is one of the weakest things to ask of a model. A model rarely answers "I don't know" unprompted. Faced with a question it has no basis to answer, it will usually emit the most probable continuation anyway, in the same confident tone-of-voice it uses for everything else. We must treat any LLM output that reads like confident recall, eg. a date, a citation, an API signature, or a quoted figure, as needing independent verification.

Because hallucination is intrinsic to the technology, a model cannot be relied on to check its own work, because its judgment of its output is subject to the same flaws as the output itself. Correctness MUST therefore be established by something that does not share those flaws: a human, or a deterministic gate. Output MUST NOT be trusted on the model's confidence alone. Where the validation can be reduced to a rule, prefer a deterministic check to a second opinion from another model.

Tacit knowledge, judgment, and accountability cannot be delegated. Deciding what is worth building, weighing long-term trade-offs, pushing back on a requirement, and answering for the outcome remain human responsibilities. A model may inform these decisions. It MUST NOT make them.

Actions that are irreversible or that have a large blast radius MUST receive proportionately more human scrutiny. These include changes touching authentication, money, personal data, safety-critical functions, or production infrastructure. The harder a mistake is to undo, the weaker the case for handing the task to a tool whose output you would have to check line-by-line anyway.

Be wary of overusing AIs to the extent that you will erode skills that must remain with humans. Delegating the fundamentals you are still learning, or that your team must retain to supervise the tools at all, trades a short-term gain for a long-term loss. Judgment and taste are built by doing the work. A team that has outsourced all the underlying work loses its ability to tell good output from bad.

Finally, data must not leave your control. Every prompt sent to a third-party model is data leaving your system. It may carry code, personal data, or secrets into a system whose retention and training practices you do not govern, and it may breach confidentiality, licensing, or data-protection obligations.
