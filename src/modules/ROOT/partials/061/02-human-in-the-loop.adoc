= Human-in-the-loop

.Key requirements
****
* A human MUST be in the loop whenever AI tools are used for software development, maintenance, or operations.
****

Projects to build software systems succeed or fail on critical choices that AI tools — at least in their 2026 frontier form — cannot make for us. These choices fall into three categories.

The first is *tacit knowledge* that lives in people's heads and in conversations, not in any artifact that an xref:garden::agent.adoc[*agent*] can retrieve. This includes: understanding of the problem space and business domain, which is unique to every software system; understanding of how a subsystem interacts with other ones outside of its subdomain; and context about why certain patterns were chosen in the past, and why others were rejected.

The second is *judgment*. This is about weighing trade-offs that have no correct answer. Judgment includes deciding what is worth building in the first place, when to push back on requirements, how much technical debt to accept against long-term maintainability, and when something is good enough to call "done". It also includes creativity, which is fundamentally about the ability to make unconventional semantic connections.

The third is xref:garden::accountability.adoc[*accountability*]. When a system causes harm, someone needs to be answerable for it — professionally, legally, and ethically. An AI tool, like any computer program, cannot hold that responsibility.

For these reasons, there MUST always be a human-in-the-loop whenever AI tools are used to support the development, maintenance, and operation of software. The objective is not to design autonomous replacements for human decision-making, but to *augment* existing software delivery processes, adding AI tools deliberately and carefully to the parts of the pipeline where they best complement our own expertise, knowledge, and judgment. And humans, not agents, direct the execution of those tools, remaining firmly in control of the environments in which AIs operate.

*Critical thinking* becomes more important with the advent of AI tools, not less. An AI tool that produces fluent, confident, plausible output, and that leans toward telling you what you want to hear, requires the human-in-the-loop to question what looks right, notice what is missing, weigh whether the approach is sound, and reject work that does not hold up. The more capable the AI model, the more this matters, because its output will be more persuasive and its mistakes better disguised.

Model providers are making genuine progress on training models to recognize their own fallibility, for example to express uncertainty in their outputs, ask clarifying questions rather than guess, and admit mistakes instead of defending them. An AI that is trained to reply "I don't know" is a safer coworker. Yet no advance in AI capability or self-awareness will substitute for human judgment, tacit knowledge, and accountability.

How much of the work is handed off to agents, and how tightly that work is supervised, should be calibrated to the domain, the risk profile of each project, and the expertise of the technical team. *Vibe coding*, in which no code is human-checked, is well suited to experiments and throwaway prototypes, and anything that doesn't need to be production-grade. At the other end of the spectrum, code that handles authentication, money, personal data, or safety-critical functions — or any change that will be hard to reverse — warrants proportionately closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line independent verification of AI-generated artifacts.

Within those outer bounds, the degree of human supervision will change over time. As confidence in an AI-augmented workflow's reliability grows, demonstrated by a track record of consistently high-quality outcomes, the workflow's human checkpoints can be deliberately reduced. Conversely, if a workflow stops producing good outcomes, that is the signal to put more human checkpoints back into the loop.

None of this displaces established engineering practice. On the contrary, agents produce changes faster and in greater volume than any human team can, so the methods and tools that keep a codebase trustworthy — clear requirements, small and reviewable changes, automated testing, version control, code review, etc. — carry more of the load in AI-augmented workflows than before.

The more of our work that is delegated to agents, the more rigorous our software development processes need to be, particularly around quality control and change management.
