= Human-in-the-loop

.Key requirements
****
* A human MUST be in the loop whenever AI tools are used for software development, maintenance, or operations.
****

Projects to build software systems succeed or fail on critical choices that AI tools — at least in their 2026 frontier form — cannot make for us. These choices fall into three categories.

The first is *tacit knowledge* that lives in people's heads and in conversations, not in any artifact that an xref:garden::agent.adoc[*agent*] can retrieve. This includes: understanding of the problem space and business domain, which is unique to every software system; understanding of how a subsystem interacts with other ones outside of its  subdomain; and context about why certain patterns were chosen in the past, and why others were rejected.

The second is *judgment*. This is about weighing trade-offs that have no correct answer. Judgment includes deciding what is worth building in the first place, when to push back on requirements, and what "done" means. It also includes: decisions about long-term maintainability and technical debt trade-offs; knowing when something is good enough, and when to stop; and creativity, which is fundamentally about the ability the make unconventional semantic connections.

The third is xref:garden::accountability.adoc[*accountability*]. When a system causes harm, someone needs to be answerable for it — professionally, legally, and ethically. An AI tool, like any computer program, cannot hold that responsibility.

For these reasons, there MUST always be a human-in-the-loop whenever AI tools are used to support the development, maintenance, and operation of software. The objective is not to design autonomous replacements for human decision-making, but to *augment* existing software delivery processes with AI tools. AI tools are deliberately, carefully added to the parts of software delivery pipelines where they are best placed to augment our own expertise, knowledge, and judgment. And humans, not agents, direct the execution of those tools, remaining firmly in control of the environments in which AIs operate.

Furthermore, *critical thinking* becomes more important with the advent of AI tools, not less. An AI tool that produces fluent, confident, plausible output, and that leans toward telling you what you want to hear, puts a greater burden of judgment on the human. The human-in-the-loop is required to review AI output, to question what looks right, notice what is missing, weigh whether the approach is sound, and reject work that does not hold up. The more capable the AI model, the more this matters, because its output will be more persuasive and its mistakes better disguised.

Model providers are making genuine progress on training models to recognize their own fallibility, for example to express calibrated uncertainty, ask clarifying questions rather than guess, and admit mistakes instead of defending them. A model that is trained to reply "I don't know" is a safer collaborator. Yet no degree of self-awareness engineered into a model can substitute for keeping a human-in-the-loop. Advancements in AI capabilities will never substitute for human judgment, tacit knowledge, and accountability.

The question becomes what is the appropriate _degree_ of AI involvement in software delivery? Or, where in the delivery pipeline do AI tools take over from tasks previously done by humans?

How much of the work is handed off to agents, and how tightly that work is supervised, should be calibrated to the domain and the risk profile of each project. *Vibe coding* is well suited to experiments and throwaway prototypes, and anything that doesn't need to be production-grade. At the other end of the spectrum, code that handles authentication, money, personal data, or safety-critical functions — or any change that will be hard to reverse — warrants proportionately closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line independent verification of AI-generated artifacts.

Within those outer bounds, the degree of human supervision is not fixed. The appropriate degree of human supervision will depend on the domain, the risk profile of the business, and the expertise of the technical team. It will also change over time. As confidence in a given AI-augmented workflow's reliability grows, demonstrated by a track record of consistent and predictable outcomes, the workflow's human checkpoints can be deliberately reduced. Conversely, if a workflow stops producing consistent, predictable outcomes, that is the signal to put more human checkpoints back into the loop.

None of this displaces established engineering practice. On the contrary, effective use of AI tools requires doubling down on age-old engineering principles, not to throw them out. Agents produce changes faster and in greater volume than any human team can, so the methods and tools that keep a codebase trustworthy — clear requirements, small and reviewable changes, automated testing, version control, code review, etc. — carry _even_ more of the load in AI-augmented workflows.

The more of our work that is delegated to agents, the more rigorous the software development processes need to be — particularly quality control and change management.

== Delegated versus unattended tasks

This standard uses two terms to describe how a task is handed to an agent. The difference between them is whether a human checks each run.

A *delegated* task keeps a human checkpoint somewhere in its lifecycle. A model does the work, while the humans may or may not be away-from-the-keyboard. But someone reviews the output, or approves it at a gate, before it takes effect. Most of what this standard covers is about AI delegation. It is about ways of AI working that keep humans in the loop.

An *unattended* task has no per-run human checkpoint. The model's output is acted on directly, with no review before or after each run. A human remains accountable for the task and able to intervene, but does not review individual outputs. Running a task unattended SHOULD be reserved for low-stakes, high-volume, easily reversible work, where an occasional wrong output is cheap. This category of work is not the subject of this technical standard.

In this technical standard, the word *automated* is reserved for deterministic scripts and pipelines. A delegated or unattended task driven by a model is not automated, rather it is *agentic*.
