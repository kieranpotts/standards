[#human-in-the-loop]
= Human-in-the-loop

.Key requirements
****
* A human MUST be in the loop whenever AI tools are used for software development, maintenance, or operations.
****

Projects to build software systems succeed or fail on critical choices that AI tools — at least in their 2026 frontier form — cannot make for us. These choices fall into three categories.

The first is *tacit knowledge* that lives in people's heads and in conversations, not in any artifact an xref:garden::agent.adoc[*agent*] can retrieve. This includes understanding of the business domain, which is unique to every software system; understanding of how subsystems interact across the boundaries of their subdomains; and context about why certain patterns were chosen in the past — and why others were rejected.

The second is *judgment*. This is about weighing trade-offs that have no correct answer. Judgment includes deciding what is worth building in the first place, and what "done" means. It also includes decisions about long-term maintainability and technical debt trade-offs; the wisdom to push back on requirements; knowing when something is good enough, and when to stop; and creativity, which is fundamentally about the ability the make unconventional semantic connections.

The third is xref:garden::accountability.adoc[*accountability*]. When a system causes harm, someone needs to be answerable for it — legally, professionally, and ethically. A large language model, like any computer program, cannot hold that responsibility.

For these reasons, there MUST always be a human-in-the-loop whenever AI tools are used to support software development, maintenance, and operations. The objective MUST NOT be to design autonomous replacements for human decision-making, but to *augment* existing software delivery processes with AI tools. This means humans, not agents, direct the execution of AI tools, remaining firmly in control of the environments in which AIs operate. And it means that AI tools are deliberately, carefully added to the parts of the software delivery pipelines where they are best placed to augment our own expertise, knowledge, and judgment.

== Critical thinking

An AI tool that produces fluent, confident, plausible output, and that leans toward telling you what you want to hear, puts a greater burden of judgment on the human, not less.

With the advent of AI tools, critical thinking becomes more important, not less.

The human-in-the-loop is required to question what looks right, notice what is missing, weigh whether the approach is sound, and reject work that does not hold up. The more capable the AI model, the more this matters, because its output will be more persuasive and its mistakes better disguised.

== Fallibility awareness

Model providers are making genuine progress on training models to recognize their own fallibility, for example to express calibrated uncertainty, ask clarifying questions rather than guess, and admit mistakes instead of defending them. A model that is trained to reply "I don't know" is a safer collaborator.

Yet no degree of self-awareness engineered into a model can substitute for keeping a human-in-the-loop. Advancements in AI capabilities will never substitute for human judgment, tacit knowledge, and accountability.

== Calibrating human involvement

There MUST always be a human-in-the-loop when using AI tools for software development. The question becomes what is the appropriate _degree_ of AI integration, or where in the delivery pipeline do AI tools take over from tasks previously done by humans?

How much of the work is handed off to agents, and how tightly that work is supervised, SHOULD be calibrated to the domain and the risk profile of each project.

A throwaway prototype, an internal script, or an experiment can be driven with a light touch and reviewed loosely. *Vibe coding* is well suited to anything that doesn't need to be production-grade, and to research and development work with no clear end goal.

At the other end of the spectrum, code that handles authentication, money, personal data, or safety-critical functions — or any change that will be hard to reverse — warrants proportionately closer scrutiny, up to line-by-line independent verification.

Within those bounds, the degree of human supervision is not fixed. The appropriate degree of human supervision will depend on the domain, the expertise of the team, and the maturity of the software system itself. It will also change over time. As confidence in a given AI-augmented workflow's reliability grows, demonstrated by a track record of consistent, predictable outcomes, the workflow's human checkpoints can be deliberately reduced. Conversely, if a workflow stops producing consistent, predictable outcomes, that is the signal to put more human checkpoints back into the loop.

None of this displaces the normal software development life cycle. On the contrary, the more of the work that is delegated to agents, the more rigorous the development processes need to be — particularly quality control and change management.

== Delegated versus unattended tasks

Broadly, tasks can be handed to agents in one of two ways.

A *delegated* task keeps a human-in-the-loop somewhere in its lifecycle. The output is reviewed, the workflow has a checkpoint, or an engineered gates it, even though a model does all the work.

An *unattended* task has no per-run human checkpoint. The model's output is acted on directly, end to end, with no review gate before or after each run. A human remains accountable for the task and able to intervene, as <<human-in-the-loop,Human-in-the-loop>> requires, but does not review individual outputs.

// Most of what this standard covers is delegation. Delegated tasks need not be simple: they can be complex, sophisticated, and carry real consequences if done badly — managing an investment portfolio delegated to an agent is still delegation, not automation, provided a human remains accountable for the outcome. Delegation buys back time. It does not remove the human decision from the loop (see <<human-in-the-loop,Human-in-the-loop>>).

// This is currently a small category, reserved for low-stakes, high-volume, easily-reversible work where an occasional wrong output is cheap — spam filtering is a familiar example, and one many people already rely on without realizing an AI model makes the call. Promoting a task from delegated to unattended is a deliberate decision to remove the per-run human checkpoint entirely, not merely to loosen it, and it SHOULD be reserved for tasks that can tolerate being wrong occasionally with no meaningful consequence. The rest of this section covers loosening or tightening supervision on tasks that remain delegated. This standard reserves the word _automated_ for deterministic scripts (see <<agentic-versus-automated,Agentic versus automated>>), so an unattended model-driven task is not called automated.
