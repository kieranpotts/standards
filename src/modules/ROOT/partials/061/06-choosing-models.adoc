= Choosing models

The return on investment from an AI tool depends less on the underlying model's raw capability than on the fit between the task and what the model is good at. Using the most capable model for every task is tempting, but naive. Every model is the product of design trade-offs, so each category of model is strong at some kinds of task and weak at others.

== Training-data coverage

Many models are optimized for particular tasks, such as computer programming or security testing.

The first question to ask when choosing a coding model is whether it has been trained on relevant examples of computer program code.

A model's knowledge is bounded by its training data. When training LLMs, AI companies download millions of examples of software code from sources like GitHub, and may optimize the models for coding tasks through a process known as *fine-tuning*. The frontier models that power most coding harnesses are general-purpose models trained on a large share of code.

But no amount of fine-tuning overcomes gaps in the underlying training data. Where that data is biased toward certain languages, frameworks, or programming styles, the model will reflect those biases in its outputs.

The practical effect is that today's AI coding agents are good at generating code in modern, well-represented languages like JavaScript and HTML, but poor at things like 6502 Assembly and niche domain-specific languages. They simply don't have the depth of semantic associations for those languages encoded in their parameters.

The cheapest way to improve a model's capabilities in an under-represented domain is to feed the missing knowledge into the context. Instruction set references, grammar specifications, API documentation, and a handful of canonical, known-good examples will give the model material to work. Extend these guides with external verification checks — eg. a compiler, automated tests — will improve outcomes further by giving the model self-verifiable success criteria that it can iterate toward.

The more expensive, but ultimately more effective, option is to fine-tune an xref:garden::open-weight-model.adoc[*open-weight model*] on your own data. This addresses the root cause rather than working around it, but it requires a substantial corpus, ML expertise, lots of compute, and repeated work on every base model upgrade.

The supply of training data is itself a limit on how far models can advance, so do not assume that model capability will keep improving at its recent pace, or uniformly across domains. Under-represented languages and niche domains are the most exposed, because there was never much data for them to begin with and little new data is being created.

== Specialist versus general-purpose models

*General-purpose models* are adequate for many tasks in modern programming languages. But for more specialized tasks you may get better results from more specialized models.

*Specialist models* are fine-tuned for a narrow domain rather than general-purpose use — a search-grounded model tuned for factual retrieval with citations, for example, or a code-completion model tuned for fill-in-the-middle IDE autocomplete.

The frontier models take most of the media attention, but a good deal of the more interesting work in the field is happening in quieter, more niche corners. One of these is the development of small, deliberately limited specialist models — models that are not trying to be capable of everything, only of one bounded job. A model that does nothing but answer customer-service questions about a particular product need not carry the weight of a general-purpose frontier model, and it is correspondingly cheap to run. Narrowing the remit is a feature, not a shortcoming.

Prefer a specialist model over a general-purpose one where the task matches its specialization closely.

Specialization is not the same thing as capability. A specialist model may be small and cheap, and still outperform a frontier model within its domain, because the associations it needs are encoded in its parameters and the frontier model's are spread thinner.

No amount of clever phrasing will substitute for associations that the model does not hold. Where the associations are missing, the remedy is to supply the knowledge in the context, or to choose a model that has it.

== Capability tiers

Beyond domain fit, the next question is how much capability the task needs — and of what kind.

Models are commonly grouped into tiers that trade capability against speed and cost.

* *Frontier* models are the current state of the art from well-resourced labs (eg. Anthropic's Claude Opus, OpenAI's GPT flagship, Google's Gemini Pro). They are the most capable but also the slowest and most expensive, so they SHOULD be reserved for work where their capability changes the outcome.

* *Mid-tier* models (eg. Claude Sonnet, GPT mid-tier releases, open-weight workhorses like Llama 70B or Qwen) balance capability, speed, and cost. They are adequate for most day-to-day tasks, including a large share of coding work.

* *Light* models (eg. Claude Haiku, Gemini Flash, GPT mini) trade capability for speed and cost. They suit high-volume, latency-sensitive, or mechanical work — eg. classification, extraction, routing, simple lookups.

Reasoning is not a fourth tier. It is a separate dial that applies within any tier, set through the inference parameters that tune a model's behavior.

Different task types stress different capabilities: language quality, step-by-step reasoning, factual grounding, or raw speed. Proofreading, for example, is linguistically demanding but reasoning-light, whereas a maths word problem is the reverse. A strong general-purpose model at low xref:garden::reasoning-effort.adoc[*reasoning effort*] suits the former task, while the same model at high effort suits the latter.

Identify the dominant capability a task demands, then choose the cheapest tier that satisfies it.

=== Assigning tiers to workflow roles

Where a task is split into steps, assign each step the cheapest tier that satisfies it. Use frontier models for planning, architecture decisions, complex problem-solving, security analysis, and architectural compliance review. In a xref:garden::multi-agent-system.adoc[*multi-agent system*], that is the role of the supervisor agent. Use mid-tier or light models for executing pre-approved plans, small-scale refactoring, and routine chores. In a multi-agent system, that is the role of the xref:garden::agent-memory.adoc#_subagent[*sub-agents*]. Once a frontier model has defined a precise, step-by-step path, cheaper models are usually capable of following it, at a fraction of the cost and latency.

== Other selection criteria

Capability and tier are not the only properties that separate one model from another. Check the following before committing to a model.

* *Knowledge cutoff.* A model knows nothing about events after its training data was collected. A library released after that date, or an API changed after it, is unknown to the model or misremembered by it. Where the work depends on recent material, supply the current documentation in the context.

* *Context window.* The context window caps how much code, documentation, and conversation history a model can take in on one inference call. A larger window admits a larger task, but output quality still degrades as the window fills.

* *Modalities.* Not every model accepts images or other non-text input. Work that depends on screenshots, diagrams, or scanned documents needs a model that does.

* *Data-handling terms.* Providers differ in how long they retain prompts, whether they train on them, and where they process them. Confirm that the provider's terms are acceptable for the class of data the model will see, before any of that data is sent.

* *Version stability.* Most providers publish each model under a fixed, versioned identifier, and also under an alias that moves to the newest release. Unattended workflows and evals SHOULD use the fixed identifier, so that behavior does not change without a change on your side. Providers also retire old versions on a published schedule. Track the retirement notices for the models you depend on, and re-run your evals before moving to a replacement.

== Capability versus trustworthiness

A more capable model is not a more trustworthy one. Measured hallucination rates have been falling release over release, as model developers invest in curating training data, in post-training techniques, and, in some models, in an explicit "reflect and revise" step that lets a model reconsider part of its own output before returning it. None of this changes the underlying mechanism. A lower rate is not the absence of the failure mode, and the discipline of verification does not relax as the rate improves.

The same goes for the techniques within your own control, such as careful prompting, cross-checking against a second context, and xref:garden::retrieval-augmented-generation.adoc[*retrieval-augmented generation*], which grounds the output by fetching the real source text into the context. They reduce how often a model hallucinates. None of them stops it.

Nor does wrapping a model in an agent change the picture. An agent is no more trustworthy than the model that drives it. A harness can make an agent's outcomes more predictable, but that predictability is engineered around the model. It is not a property of the model.

== Open-weight models

Open-weight models have publicly released xref:garden::model-weights.adoc[*weights*] that can be downloaded and run on your own hardware. They can also be consumed as a service from a third-party xref:garden::inference-provider.adoc[*inference provider*], billed per token as a closed model is.

Open-weight models span the capability tiers — several of the mid-tier workhorses are open-weight — and there are open-weight specialist models too. What is being chosen here is less a level of capability than where the model runs and who holds the data it sees.

Open-weight models typically trail frontier models in capability by at least a few months. In exchange, a self-hosted open-weight model eliminates per-token cost, can run offline, and keeps your data private on infrastructure that you control. These benefits come from self-hosting, not from the open weights. An open-weight model served by a third party offers none of them.

Open-weight models, running on your own infrastructure or an enterprise cloud environment, are RECOMMENDED for privacy-sensitive or offline work, and for high-volume tasks where local hardware is adequate for the job.

But running your own weights transfers work to you. You take on the xref:garden::model-runtime.adoc[*model runtime*], the hardware, and the security of the serving endpoint.

== Benchmarks and leaderboards

Public benchmarks and leaderboards (eg. SWE-bench for coding, Terminal-Bench for agentic terminal tasks, LiveBench for contamination-resistant general capability) are a useful first filter when comparing candidate models. Cross-provider trackers (eg. Artificial Analysis, OpenRouter rankings, LMArena) give a view across labs. They do not all measure the same thing. OpenRouter's rankings, for example, order models by the tokens processed through its API, which shows adoption and not capability. Check what a ranking measures before relying on it.

Read a public score with three caveats. A model may have seen the benchmark's tasks in its training data, which inflates its score. A benchmark on which the leading models all score near the maximum no longer separates them. And many published scores are reported by the model's own vendor, measured with a harness and settings of the vendor's choosing.

But public benchmarks are no substitute for evaluating candidate models against your own representative tasks.
