= AI adoption

.Key requirements
****
* Audit trails MUST be explicitly engineered into AI workflows.
* Proprietary code and data MUST NOT be given to a tool that the organization's AI policy does not approve.
* Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human or team owner.
****

There are enormous benefits to be had from adopting AI tools in software delivery. But those benefits must be balanced against the inherent costs and risks in AI tools. Some of those costs and risks accrue from the pattern of adoption across teams and over time. They include, but are not limited to, the following.

* *Growing dependence.* As workflows are built around AI tools, the organization comes to increasingly rely on their continued availability, pricing, and capability. A model that is deprecated, repriced, or degraded upstream is a vendor risk like any other. Treat concentration on a single vendor as a risk to be managed.

* *Loss of knowledge and skill.* A team that delegates the fundamentals of software delivery to AI tools gradually loses the expertise it needs to supervise them. Guarding against this requires a deliberate policy about which skills the team must keep for itself, and which it may delegate to AIs.

* *No audit trail.* Out-of-the-box, most agentic systems leave no durable account of the workloads they ran, what data passed through them, and what the outcomes were. Audit trails MUST be explicitly engineered into AI workflows.

* *Copyright and intellectual property.* A model can, on occasion, reproduce or closely derive from copyrighted material in its training data, so generated output carries a residual risk of infringing someone else's rights. The risk is usually small, but it grows with the volume of AI-generated artifacts. Review generated output for verbatim reproduction of third-party material, and check what indemnity the vendor's terms offer against infringement claims.

* *Declining delivery stability.* Faster code production does not automatically translate into faster or safer software delivery. The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) program's 2024 report estimated that each 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with a 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. By 2025 the throughput association had turned positive, but the association with instability persisted. One possible explanation is that more code, produced faster, raises batch size, so each deployment is larger and riskier. Speeding up one part of the delivery pipeline only moves the bottleneck elsewhere — so look to augment the whole delivery lifecycle with AI tools.

Weighing these costs and risks against the benefits is not a judgment to be left to individual developers or teams. It requires policy that is set at the level of the organization. An organization that uses AI tools SHOULD record a policy that states, at least, which models, harnesses, and services may be used, for which categories of task, and for which classes of data. A tool that the policy does not approve MUST NOT be given proprietary code or data.

AI policies SHOULD also state whether and how AI authorship is disclosed in commits and pull requests, eg. with a commit trailer or a pull request label. Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human or team owner. For an interactive session, the owner is the developer who ran it, and for an unattended workflow or a loop, the owner is the person or team that operates the loop.
