= AI adoption

.Key requirements
****
* The audit record of what an agent was asked and what it did MUST be engineered into a workflow.
* Proprietary code and data MUST NOT be given to a tool that is not on the approved list.
* Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human owner.
****

== Costs of adoption

Some costs of AI tools do not show up in any single task. They accrue from the pattern of adoption across a team and over time. They are easy to discount, because none of them causes an obvious failure on the day it takes root.

* *Growing dependence.* As workflows are built around AI tools, the organization comes to rely on their continued availability, pricing, and capability, none of which it controls. A model that is deprecated, repriced, or degraded upstream is a dependency risk like any other, but one whose reach grows with every workflow that assumes it. Prefer designs that keep the tool substitutable, and that degrade to a human or a deterministic path rather than stall. Treat concentration on a single provider as a risk to be managed.

* *Loss of knowledge and skill.* The erosion of skill is a cost to the team as well as to the individual. A team that delegates the fundamentals loses the expertise that makes supervision of the tools possible. Guarding against this takes a deliberate policy about which skills the team keeps sharp, regardless of what the tools can do.

* *No audit trail.* Unless a workflow is designed to record it, there is no durable account of what an agent was asked, what it did, and what data passed through it. That undermines incident investigation, accountability, and any obligation to demonstrate compliance. The record does not exist by default. It MUST be engineered in.

* *Copyright and intellectual property.* A model can, on occasion, reproduce or closely derive from copyrighted material in its training data, so generated output carries a residual risk of infringing someone else's rights. The risk is usually small, and it is highest where the model reproduces a distinctive body of work at length. The underlying legal question of whether training a model on copyrighted work is itself lawful is unsettled, and it is being answered differently across jurisdictions. The exposure grows with how much generated material a team ships unreviewed and unattributed. Treat human review and, where it matters, provenance checking as the mitigation.

* *Declining delivery stability.* Faster code production does not automatically translate into faster or safer delivery. The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) program's 2024 research associated each 25% increase in AI adoption with an estimated 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability, even as individual coding productivity rose. Its 2025 research found the throughput association had turned positive, while the association with instability persisted. More code, produced faster, raises batch size and risk, and pushes maintenance costs downstream. Treat throughput and stability, not lines of code or task-completion speed, as the metrics that decide whether an AI adoption effort is working.

None of these is an argument against adoption. Each is a cost to be weighed and managed openly, so that the reach of the tools grows by decision rather than by default.

== Team policy

Adoption by decision requires the decisions to be written down. A team that uses AI tools SHOULD record a policy that covers at least the following.

* *Approved tools.* Which models, harnesses, and services may be used, and for which classes of data. A tool that is not on the list MUST NOT be given proprietary code or data.

* *Disclosure.* Whether and how AI authorship is disclosed in commits and pull requests, eg. with a commit trailer or a pull request label. Disclosure tells a reviewer where to direct their scrutiny. It does not transfer responsibility to the tool.

* *Accountability.* Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human owner, who answers for it exactly as for a change written by hand. For an interactive session, the owner is the developer who ran it. For an unattended workflow or a loop, the owner is the person or team that operates it.

* *Retained skills.* Which skills the team keeps in practice, regardless of what the tools can do.

Review the policy whenever a tool, a provider's terms, or the team's risk profile changes.
