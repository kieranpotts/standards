= AI adoption

.Key requirements
****
* The audit record of what an agent was asked and what it did MUST be engineered into a workflow.
* Proprietary code and data MUST NOT be given to a tool that is not on the approved list.
* Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human owner.
****

There are enormous benefits to be had from adopting AI tools in software delivery. But those benefits must be balanced against the inherent costs and risks in AI tools. Some of those costs and risks accrue from the pattern of adoption across teams and over time. They include, but are not limited, to the following.

* *Growing dependence.* As workflows are built around AI tools, the organization comes to inreasingly rely on their continued availability, pricing, and capability. A model that is deprecated, repriced, or degraded upstream is a dependency/vendor risk like any other. Treat concentration on a single vendor as a risk to be managed.

* *Loss of knowledge and skill.* A team that delegates the fundamentals of software delivery to AI tools will gradually lose the expertise that is required to properly supervise those tools. Guarding against this requires a deliberate policy about which skills the team must keep for itself, and which it may delegate to AIs.

* *No audit trail.* Out-of-the-box, most agentic systems leave no durable account of the workloads they ran, what data passed through them, and what the outcomes were. Audit trails MUST be explcitly engineered into AI workflows.

* *Copyright and intellectual property.* A model can, on occasion, reproduce or closely derive from copyrighted material in its training data, so generated output carries a residual risk of infringing someone else's rights. The risk is usually small, but the risk grows with the volume of AI-generated artifacts.

* *Declining delivery stability.* Faster code production does not automatically translate into faster or safer software delivery. The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) program's 2024 research paper found that each 25% increase in AI adoption produced an average 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. By 2025 the research had found that the throughput association had turned positive, but the association with instability persisted. Why the negative correlation between speed of code generation and speed and stability of software delivery? One possible explanation is that more code, produced faster, raises batch size, meaning we're deploying larger, and therefore riskier, software updates. Optimization of one part of the delivery pipeline simply leads to new bottlenecks in other parts, both upstream and downstream. We must instead look to augment the whole delivery lifecycle with AI tools, accelerating delivery throughput while maintaining delivery stability.

Weighing these costs and risks against the benefits is not a judgment to be left to individual developers or teams. It requires policy that is set at the level of the organization. An organization that uses AI tools SHOULD record a policy that states, at least, which models, harnesses, and services may be used, for which categories of task, and for which classes of data. A tool that is not on the approved list MUST NOT be given proprietary code or data.

AI policies SHOULD also state whether and how AI authorship is disclosed in commits and pull requests, eg. with a commit trailer or a pull request label. Every change that an agent produces MUST have a named human owner. For an interactive session, the owner is the developer who ran it, and for an unattended workflow or a loop, the owner is the person or team that operates the loop.
