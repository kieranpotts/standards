= Usability

Usability is the measure of how well a system serves the people who use it: whether they can achieve what they came to
do, at an acceptable cost in time and effort, without the system frustrating them on the way. Those three concerns —
effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction — are the conventional decomposition of the quality, and they are the
reason it is measurable where "user experience" is not. Task completion rates, time on task, and error rates are all
observable quantities.

Usability is the external quality that users perceive most directly, and the other four largely reach them through it.
A system that is functionally suitable but slow to answer, correct but incomprehensible, or reliable but unforgiving of
mistakes, is a system whose other qualities its users never get the benefit of.

It does not follow that usability is merely the sum of the other four, or that it can be left to look after itself once
they are in hand. A system can be functionally suitable, correct, fast, and reliable and still be unusable, because
usability makes demands that none of the others make. It has to be learnable by someone meeting it for the first time,
forgiving of the mistakes those people will make, and operable by people whose abilities and circumstances differ from
the designers' own. Each of those has to be designed for on its own terms, and each is the subject of a section below,
along with responsiveness, which is the constituent that most shapes how a system feels to use.

Two further constituents of usability are covered elsewhere in this technical standard, because they are properties of
other qualities that happen to surface here. Predictable behavior when things go wrong belongs to
<<reliability,reliability>>. The interface consistency that lets a user transfer what they have learned in one part of
a system to another belongs to <<simplicity,simplicity>>.

[NOTE]
======
{link-iso-25010}[ISO/IEC 25010] splits this material across its two models, and the vocabulary is worth untangling. In
the product quality model, the characteristic is *interaction capability*: the attributes of a design that let people
interact with it, covering appropriateness recognizability, learnability, operability, user error protection, user
engagement, inclusivity, user assistance, and self-descriptiveness. *Usability* is reserved for the quality-in-use
model, where it names the outcome — effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction for a given user in a given context.
Interaction capability is a prerequisite for usability rather than a synonym for it.

This technical standard uses "usability" for both, in its ordinary sense, on the grounds that the outcome is the thing
worth naming the quality after. A reader arriving from xref:001.adoc[*TS-1: Software requirements specification*],
which adopts the ISO taxonomy for grouping quality requirements, will find this chapter's subject listed there under
interaction capability. Note also that the 2011 edition of ISO/IEC 25010 called the product characteristic "usability"
and treated accessibility as a sub-characteristic of it. The 2023 edition renamed the characteristic and absorbed
accessibility into inclusivity.
======

== Responsiveness

Among all the factors that contribute to usability, one stands out as particularly critical. *Responsiveness.*

Responsiveness is not about how fast the software actually operates in objective terms. Rather, it's about how fast the
software _feels_ to the user. This perception is shaped by the feedback the system provides to the user. Feedback should
arrive quickly and it should clearly communicate what is happening. When feedback is delayed or absent, users perceive
the software as slow and unresponsive, even if the actual computation is happening quickly in the background.
Conversely, well-designed feedback can make operations that take considerable time feel fast and responsive because the
user is constantly informed of progress.

Fast, responsive software changes the behavior of its users. It reduces the cognitive friction involved in using the
software, allowing users to stay focused on their tasks, and so to be more productive. Conversely, slow software limits
us, perhaps more than most people realize. Think, for example, when was the last time you got any meaningful work done
on airplane Wi-Fi?

In order to make software responsive, you often have to do complex things behind the scenes. Instagram, for example,
optimistically uploads users' photos while they are still typing the captions for them, so the subsequent user-initiated
upload operation feels instantaneous.

Fast, reliable software has a competitive advantage, too. Over time, the perceived speed of Instagram has no doubt
played a big role in its success, relative to the many other photo sharing apps that competed in the early days.

Responsiveness also shapes how reliable a system is taken to be. Software that answers promptly, and that handles its
errors gracefully, is read as sound; software that stalls is read as broken, whatever its actual failure record.
<<reliability,Reliability>> treats that same perception from the other side.

== Learnability

A system is learnable to the degree that someone meeting it for the first time can build a working mental model of it
quickly, and to the degree that the model they build turns out to be right. Both halves matter. A system that is easy
to form a confident but mistaken impression of is worse than one that is obviously unfamiliar, because the user has no
signal telling them to slow down.

Learnability is largely downstream of <<conceptual-integrity,conceptual integrity>>. Where a system is built from one
set of ideas applied consistently, a user learns the idea once and can then predict how it applies everywhere else.
Where a system is an accumulation of unreconciled ideas, each area has to be learned separately, and nothing learned in
one place transfers to another. This is the same property that makes a codebase navigable for developers, showing up on
the other side of the interface.

Before a user can learn a system, they have to recognize that it is a system for them at all. Whether the purpose of a
product is apparent from a first encounter — whether a user can tell that it does the job they came to do — is a design
concern in its own right, and one that is usually settled by the first screen rather than by anything further in.

Where the design cannot be made self-evident, the gap is closed with user assistance: onboarding, contextual help, and
documentation. This is a fallback and should be recognized as one. Documentation that exists because the design could
not be made obvious is a cost the user pays, in the same way that a comment explaining a confusing function is a cost
the next developer pays. It is worth paying when the alternative is worse, and worth removing when the design can be
fixed instead.

== User error protection

<<reliability,Reliability>> is concerned with the ways a system can fail. Usability is concerned with the ways its
users can, and the two need different designs. A user error is rarely a fault in any component: the system does exactly
what it was asked, and what it was asked was wrong.

The strongest form of protection is to make the error impossible to express. An input that cannot represent an invalid
value cannot carry one, a control that is unavailable cannot be operated at the wrong moment, and an operation that is
scoped to the current selection cannot be applied to the wrong records. This is the interface-level counterpart of the
argument made under <<functional-correctness,correctness>> for constraining state so that invalid states are
unrepresentable, and it is preferable to detection for the same reason: an error that cannot occur needs no handling,
no message, and no recovery path.

Where an error can be expressed, the next line of defence is to make it recoverable. Undo is the general mechanism, and
it is worth a great deal more than a confirmation dialog, which users learn to dismiss without reading. Reserve
confirmation for operations that genuinely cannot be undone, and design deliberately for how few of those there are:
a deletion that is retained for thirty days is not irreversible, and does not need to be guarded as though it were.

Where an error can neither be prevented nor undone, what remains is the message. A useful one says what happened, what
the consequence is, and what the user can do next. Messages that report the internal condition rather than the user's
situation — a constraint name, a status code, a stack frame — fail all three tests, and are a common symptom of error
handling written for the developer rather than for the person on the other side of it.

== Inclusivity

Inclusivity is the degree to which a system can be used by people across the full range of abilities, circumstances,
and equipment it is meant to serve. Accessibility, in the sense of use by people with disabilities, is the largest part
of it, but not the whole: connection quality, device capability, language, and domain literacy all determine whether a
given person can get the system to do what they need.

Inclusivity is an architectural concern rather than a presentation-layer one, and it behaves like the other
architecturally significant requirements described under <<runtime-quality,runtime quality>>. Semantic document
structure, keyboard operability, focus management, and the availability of text alternatives are consequences of how
the component model and the markup were designed. They are cheap to build in and expensive to retrofit, because
retrofitting them means revisiting every component rather than adding a layer on top.

Unlike much of usability, inclusivity is precisely specifiable. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines define
testable success criteria at graded conformance levels, so an inclusivity requirement can be written as an acceptance
criterion in the same form as a latency budget — see xref:001.adoc[*TS-1: Software requirements specification*], which
gives "WCAG 2.2 Level AA" as a worked example of exactly this. Much of it can be verified automatically.

It is frequently a legal obligation as well as a design goal. Public-sector procurement in the European Union, and
equivalent regimes elsewhere, make conformance a condition of sale. Where that applies, inclusivity is a compliance
constraint of the kind the runtime quality chapter describes, and carries the same consequence: it has to be specified
before the architecture is settled, not discovered during an audit.

== Attention to detail

Usability is fundamentally about *attention to detail* in the design. Attention to detail must extend beyond the
user interface. Every architectural layer of a system impacts the user's experience. Design decisions made in database
query efficiency, caching strategies, API design, network communication patterns, and deployment infrastructure, all
ultimately show through in the user experience.

To achieve truly fast and responsive software requires conscious design decisions throughout the entire system, not just
at the presentation layer. The most beautifully designed interface becomes frustrating if the back-end is sluggish.
Conversely, a fast back-end can be undermined by inefficient front-end code.

This is what makes usability a design quality rather than a finishing stage. It cannot be delegated to the layer
nearest the user, because the constraints that determine it are set much deeper than that, and by the time the
interface is being built most of them have already been decided. Responsiveness, learnability, error protection, and
inclusivity all have to be treated as first-class architectural concerns, not as afterthoughts to be addressed once the
functionality is complete.
