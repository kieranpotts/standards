This technical standard sets out a philosophy for software design, built around nine core qualities for evaluating
a software system's design. In no particular order, these qualities are the following.

1.  *Functional suitability.* Whether a system provides the right functions.
2.  *Correctness.* Whether a system's behavior matches its specification, and its state stays valid.
3.  *Runtime quality.* How well a system meets the non-functional constraints it operates under.
4.  *Reliability.* A system's ability to handle and recover from failures of all kinds.
5.  *Usability.* How well a system serves the people who use it.
6.  *Habitability.* What it is like for developers to work in a system's code.
7.  *Conceptual integrity.* To what degree a system's design forms a unified, consistent whole.
8.  *Changeability.* The ease with which software can be adapted to changing requirements.
9.  *Simplicity.* How well a system minimizes unnecessary complexity in both its interface and implementation.

These software qualities are universal. They have an impact at every abstraction level within a system's architecture,
and they are relevant to all types of software – whatever the runtime environment, whatever the programming language,
and whether the system is distributed or single-node.

No priority should be assumed from the order in which these qualities are presented. Their relative importance will
differ between business domains and architectural styles. But _all_ these qualities are of importance in _every_
software system to some degree.

The nine qualities divide into two groups. Qualities 1 to 5 are external. They are observable at runtime, in the way a
system behaves for its users in production, and they are the qualities a business specifies as requirements. Qualities 6
to 9 are internal. They are properties of the design itself, visible to the people who build and maintain a system
rather than to the people who use it.

It's best not to view these qualities as being mutually exclusive. In fact, there's much overlap between them and they
reinforce one another. For example, the design constraints necessary to make a system fast and responsive tend also to
result in system designs that are simple, easy to change, and fault-tolerant.

Taken as a whole, these quality attributes form a coherent software design philosophy. The objective of this technical
standard is to help software architects to reason about the trade-offs in a system design, where qualities compete with
one another for priority. Making trade-offs is the subject of the final section of this technical standard.

This technical standard also touches upon the software design _process_ itself. Topics such as iterative and incremental
development, feedback loops, and domain modeling are mentioned. After all, these are important methods and tools to help
us achieve our design goals. But the focus of this technical standard is the end result – what good software
architecture looks like – not the means of getting there.
