= Functional suitability

Functional suitability is the degree to which a system provides functions that meet the stated and implied needs of its
users, under the conditions it is specified to operate in. It answers the most basic question that can be asked of a
software system, which is whether it does the job it was built to do.

{link-iso-25010}[ISO/IEC 25010] divides functional suitability into three sub-characteristics.

* *Functional completeness.* Whether the set of functions covers all of the specified tasks and user objectives. This
  is coverage of the current delivered specification. A gap between what a system does and what its specification says
  its users need to accomplish is a defect to be closed.

* *Functional correctness.* Whether those functions produce correct results, to the degree of precision the requirements
  call for.

* *Functional appropriateness.* Whether the functions specified are the ones that actually accomplish the user's
  objectives. A system can cover its specification exactly and still be worth nothing to the people using it, if those
  were the wrong requirements in the first place.

<<correctness,Correctness>> is substantial enough to warrant a chapter of its own, and it follows this one. It is also
drawn wider there than ISO 25010 draws it. ISO's functional correctness is a property of the results a function
returns, whereas this standard's correctness extends to the validity of the state left behind, which ISO distributes
across reliability and security. This chapter covers the other two sub-characteristics, and the relationship between
them, which is that completeness is worth nothing without appropriateness.

== Functional completeness

There is an obvious objection to measuring functional completeness at all. Much modern software – especially web-based
software-as-a-service and other continuously-delivered systems – is perpetual work-in-progress, continuously
evolving in response to ever-changing requirements. Such products are never truly _complete_.

But that objection mistakes what completeness is measured against. It is measured against the requirements agreed for
the release in hand, not against everything the product might one day do. A continuously-delivered product has a
specification at every release point. What changes is that the specification keeps moving, which makes completeness a
moving target rather than an absent one. At any given moment a system either covers the requirements currently agreed
for it, or it does not.

What _does_ follow from continuous delivery is that specifying a whole system up-front and building to it is a poor way
to arrive at completeness. Best practice is to target a small but critical subset of functionality for the first release
– a minimal viable product (MVP) – and to hold that release to the same standard, complete against its own narrow
specification. From that baseline the system's capabilities can be incremented, and its design iterated to fit, driven
by user feedback.

== The New Jersey style

This is consistent with Richard P Gabriel's principle of {link-rise-of-worse-is-better}[*worse-is-better*]. This design
philosophy states that it is better to start with a minimal creation that solves the customer's core problem, while
leaving rough edges that will require refinement in the future.

The concept of worse-is-better originated in a famous talk by Gabriel at the 1989 European Conference on the Practical
Applications of Lisp. In that talk, Gabriel described two contrasting software design philosophies.

* *The MIT/Stanford approach (the "right thing")*, which prioritizes correctness, consistency, completeness, and
  simplicity, with a strong emphasis on doing things properly, even if doing so adds complexity.

* *The New Jersey style ("worse-is-better")*, which emphasizes simplicity in implementation over simplicity of the
  interface, and tolerates some inconsistencies and incompleteness if that makes the system easier to build and
  maintain.

In the talk, Gabriel suggested that Unix and C were examples of the worse-is-better design philosophy. These were not
superior technologies from a technical perspective, Gabriel argued, but nonetheless they were outperforming (and
outliving) more elegant, but more complex, systems like Lisp ("the right thing").

== Iterative and incremental development

This approach of starting with a narrow featureset and a simple design, and from there incrementing that featureset and
iterating the design driven by user feedback, is best achieved through *iterative and incremental development*.

Iterative and incremental development is a process model in which software is built through repeated cycles of
development, with each cycle delivering small, working incremental changes.

This process enables short feedback loops with real users, allowing requirements to emerge and the design to evolve in
lockstep.

Stripped of its methodological trappings, the iterative and incremental loop is very simple. Progress comes from
repeatedly assessing the current state, moving a short distance towards a goal, and revising your understanding in light
of the result. Then repeat.

[quote, Dave Thomas]
____
Find out where you are. +
Take a small step towards your goal. +
Adjust your understanding based on what you learned. +
Repeat.
____

Iterative and incremental development processes are especially effective at delivering complete, correct software
because the development and testing of the evolving, working software supports the discovery of its requirements.
Requirements gathered before any software exists are, at best, informed guesses. Users are poor predictors of their own
future behavior, and stakeholders routinely discover what they _actually_ want only by reacting to something concrete.
So, the only reliable way to find out a project's true requirements is to put working software into the hands of real
users, and observe how they respond to it.

[quote, Martin Fowler]
____
When to use iterative development? You should use iterative development only on projects that you want to succeed.
____

== Data-driven design

Functional completeness is coverage of the specification, which means the whole of functional suitability rests on the
specification being the right one. Appropriateness is the harder of the two to establish. Coverage can be checked
against a document. Appropriateness can only be checked against behavior.

Determining which requirements are worth specifying at all is therefore an empirical question, not a matter of opinion.

*Data-driven design* is the practice of grounding design decisions in observed user behavior, rather than in opinions
and assumptions about what users need. It requires collecting and analyzing telemetry data on how the software is
actually used in the real world, and feeding that back into decisions about what to build/change/remove next.

*A/B testing* is one of numerous methods that support data-driven design. It involves comparing versions of a feature
against predefined metrics, with users randomly assigned to experience one version, to establish which performs better
on average.

Data-driven design _requires_ an iterative and incremental software delivery. Shipping small increments creates the
opportunity to learn from real usage. Instrumenting those incremental releases with A/B testing and observability
tools, for example, converts that opportunity into concrete knowledge.

Without measurement, an iterative process merely produces frequent releases without the feedback loops, because the
value delivered by each release remains unverified.

Completeness makes no claim beyond coverage. In keeping with the New Jersey style, a release that fully covers its
specification may still have a user interface that is rough around the edges, and a design that is not yet what it will
eventually be. What completeness says is that everything agreed for this release has been delivered. Whether it was
worth agreeing is the question data-driven design answers. Whether it was built well is the subject of the chapters
that follow.

== Big design up-front

The alternative approach is *big design up-front*. This tries to resolve uncertainty and ambiguity in requirements by
front-loading more analysis and specification work before construction of the software begins. The subsequent build may
still be done incrementally, allowing for stakeholders to inspect the evolving software at regular checkpoints, but the
goal is to avoid needing to deviate significantly from the original plan.

This can be a good approach in scenarios where a release cannot be easily patched in the next iteration. Safety-critical
and regulated systems, systems with irreversible or expensive migrations (eg. a core data model or a public API
contract), and systems that must satisfy a fixed external constraint (eg. a legal requirement or a hardware interface)
are all examples of domains that benefit from resolving as much uncertainty as possible before committing to an
implementation.

But no amount of analysis substitutes for contact with reality. Assumptions about user needs remain unverified until
users actually use the thing. And the cost of being wrong about the requirements compounds with every layer of design
and implementation built on top of unverified assumptions.

Validating and iterating on the requirements through delivery of working software in small increments reduces delivery
risks, by surfacing wrong assumptions made in the requirements while there is still little cost sunk into them.

All software systems benefit from some degree of big design up-front, and some degree of iterative and incremental
delivery. The relative weight given to each depends on how expensive it is to change course later. The right balance may
vary between different parts of a system design, so the two approaches are best applied selectively, part by part,
rather than adopted wholesale as a single philosophy for the entire system.

== Piecemeal growth

The New Jersey software design philosophy, supported through an iterative and incremental delivery process, is analogous
to https://www.patternlanguage.com/ca/ca.html[Christopher Alexander's] *piecemeal growth* philosophy in urban design.
Alexander's philosophy is that urban architecture that strives for functional completion and technical perfection from
the start is often less adaptable, and therefore less effective at meeting the needs of its human occupants over the
long term, compared to architecture that prioritizes simplicity and that tolerates some imperfection.

Piecemeal growth is well suited to modern software, most of which is now developed and operated continuously throughout
its lifetime. Modern software systems are not delivered as one-off _projects_ with an end date, but as _products_ that
keep evolving for as long as they are being used. At each release point, a system is _complete_ – it does what it is
meant to do, for the requirements understood at that point in time – without ever being _finished_.
